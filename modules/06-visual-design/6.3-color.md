# 6.3 Colour

## In one line

Colour is the least reliable channel for meaning and the most powerful for feeling — so use it for tone and emphasis, and never as the only carrier of information.

## Building a palette

A working system needs far fewer colours than beginners expect, and far more *steps* of each:

- **Neutrals (the workhorse)** — 8–12 steps from near-white to near-black. Most of the interface is neutral. Getting the neutral ramp right matters more than the brand colour.
- **Primary/brand** — one hue, 5–10 steps. Used for the primary action and key accents, not for decoration.
- **Semantic colours** — success, warning, danger, info. Each needs several steps (background tint, border, text, icon) to be usable at accessible contrast.
- **Optional accents** — for data visualisation and categorisation, chosen for distinguishability rather than harmony.

**Steps, not shades.** Define a numeric ramp (50, 100, …, 900) and use tokens by role (`text-primary`, `surface-raised`, `border-subtle`), not by value. Role-based tokens are what make dark mode and theming possible without rewriting every component.

## Colour models worth knowing

- **HSL** is easy to reason about but perceptually uneven: two colours with the same L can look wildly different in brightness.
- **OKLCH / LCH** are perceptually uniform, so equal lightness values *look* equally light. Generating ramps in a perceptual space produces palettes that behave predictably across hues, and it is now well supported on the web.
- **Contrast ratio** (WCAG 2.x) is computed from relative luminance, not from your perception of "darkness." Always measure; never eyeball.

## Semantic colour

| Role | Common convention | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Success | Green | Colour-blindness makes red/green the worst possible pair to distinguish |
| Warning | Amber/yellow | Very hard to make accessible on white; usually needs a dark text pair |
| Danger/destructive | Red | Also used for "recording" and, in some markets, for positive financial movement |
| Info | Blue | Frequently collides with your link and primary-action colour |

**Cultural variance is real:** in many East Asian markets red indicates a *rising* stock price and green a falling one — the inverse of Western convention. Colour meanings are learned, not universal.

**The unbreakable rule:** never encode meaning with colour alone. Pair it with an icon, a label, a pattern, or a position. This serves users with colour-vision deficiency (~8% of men), greyscale contexts, poor screens, and bright sunlight — and it makes interfaces clearer for everyone.

## Contrast requirements (see Module 10 for the full treatment)

- Body text: **4.5:1** (WCAG AA)
- Large text (≥18.66 px bold or ≥24 px): **3:1**
- UI components and graphical objects (borders of inputs, icons carrying meaning): **3:1**
- AAA: 7:1 body, 4.5:1 large

Two frequent failures: placeholder text as the only label (usually below contrast *and* a recall problem), and disabled states that are illegible — disabled still needs to be readable enough to be understood.

## Dark mode

Not an inversion. Practical rules:

- **Avoid pure black backgrounds** for large surfaces; near-black (e.g. #121212 upward) reduces halation and looks less harsh, though pure black saves power on OLED.
- **Avoid pure white text**; slightly dimmed reduces glare.
- **Elevation is expressed by lightness in dark mode**, not by shadow — higher surfaces are lighter.
- **Desaturate saturated colours** — highly saturated hues vibrate against dark backgrounds.
- **Re-check every contrast ratio.** A palette that passes in light mode routinely fails in dark.
- **Respect the system setting**, and offer an explicit override; some users need light mode for readability reasons (some forms of astigmatism make light-on-dark harder).

## Colour and emotion

Colour carries tone quickly: cool and desaturated reads as calm, professional, clinical; warm and saturated reads as energetic, playful, urgent. This is genuine and worth using — but it is a *modifier*, not a mechanism. The layout, hierarchy, and copy do the work; colour sets the register.

## Applications

**Do this / not that**
- *Not that:* A green dot for "online" and a red dot for "offline," with no label.
- *Do this:* A green filled dot with the label "Online", a hollow grey dot with "Offline" — redundant encoding through colour, fill, and text.

**Example — the accent trap.** Using the brand colour for headers, links, active states, icons, and section backgrounds simultaneously destroys its usefulness for indicating the primary action. Reserve the strongest form of the accent for one job.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "How do you build a colour palette for a product?"
- "What is wrong with this colour usage?"
- "How would you approach dark mode?"

**Model answer for palette:** "I start with the neutral ramp, because most of the interface is neutral and it determines how everything else reads. Then one brand hue with enough steps to hit accessible contrast in both themes, then semantic colours with a full set — background, border, text — because a single hex value cannot serve all three at 4.5:1. I define tokens by role rather than by value, which is what makes theming and dark mode tractable. And I check every pairing with a contrast tool rather than by eye, plus a greyscale pass to confirm nothing depends on hue alone."
