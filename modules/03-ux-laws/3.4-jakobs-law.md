# 3.4 Jakob's Law

## In one line

Users spend most of their time on *other* products, so they expect yours to work the same way.

## Basic

Jakob Nielsen's formulation. Your product is a tiny fraction of a user's total interface experience. Their expectations — where the logo goes, what a magnifying glass means, what swipe-down does, where the cart lives — were formed elsewhere and arrive pre-installed.

Following conventions is not a lack of creativity. It is spending the user's learning budget on the parts of your product that are actually novel.

## Deeper

### Convention layers

Conventions exist at different strengths, and the cost of breaking them differs:

| Layer | Examples | Cost of breaking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Platform** | Back gesture, share icon, system dialogs, keyboard shortcuts | Very high — you fight muscle memory and accessibility tooling |
| **Category** | Cart top-right, filters on the left, feed pulls to refresh | High — users compare you directly to competitors |
| **Product-internal** | Your own navigation patterns | Medium — inconsistency inside a product is worse than being unusual |
| **Decorative** | Illustration style, motion personality, tone | Low — this is where distinctiveness belongs |

**The strategic point:** be conventional in structure, distinctive in expression. Novelty in layer 4 is free; novelty in layer 1 is expensive and rarely rewarded.

### When breaking convention pays

1. The new model is meaningfully better on a dimension the user cares about.
2. The task is frequent enough that the learning cost is amortised.
3. You can afford a bridge — onboarding, a familiar fallback, or a cue that teaches in place.
4. You have a way to measure whether the bet worked.

Absent all four, take the convention.

### The redesign trap

Because expectations include *your own past behaviour*, redesigns break learned models for existing users. Metrics often dip and then recover — the "change aversion" curve. Two implications:

- Distinguish transient change aversion from a genuine regression: measure over weeks, split by tenure (new users have no old model to violate), and watch task success, not sentiment.
- Roll out progressively and preserve escape hatches where the cost is high.

### The counter-case

Jakob's Law describes expectations, not optimality. Conventions can be collectively bad — the hamburger menu spread widely and measurably reduces discoverability of the items inside it compared with visible tabs. "Everyone does it" is evidence about expectations, not evidence about effectiveness. Distinguish the two in an interview and you sound like you have read past the poster.

## Applications

- **Ecommerce**: cart top-right, product image left / details right, price near the buy button. Deviating costs conversion for no benefit.
- **Forms**: labels above fields, errors adjacent and specific, primary action bottom-right of the form (or full-width on mobile).
- **Auth**: "Forgot password?" next to the password field, not buried in a help centre.

**Do this / not that**

- *Not that:* An icon-only navigation with your own custom metaphors because the standard icons "look generic."
- *Do this:* Standard icons with labels, and put the distinctiveness into typography, colour, motion, and content voice.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "How do you balance innovation with familiarity?"
- "Our competitor does X, should we copy it?"
- "How do you handle users complaining after a redesign?"

**For "should we copy the competitor?"** — "Copying the *structure* is usually right; users' expectations were built there. Copying the *solution* is only right if we have evidence it works — competitors ship untested things constantly. I would separate the two: adopt the convention, then verify whether their specific implementation actually performs, because 'they do it' is evidence about expectation, not about effectiveness."

**For redesign complaints** — name change aversion explicitly, then say how you would tell it apart from a real regression: segment by user tenure, track task success rather than sentiment, watch the curve over 4–6 weeks, and pre-commit to a rollback threshold.
