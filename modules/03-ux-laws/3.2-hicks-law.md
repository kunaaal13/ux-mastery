# 3.2 Hick's Law

## In one line

Decision time grows logarithmically with the number of equally likely choices — so more options slow people down, but with diminishing severity.

## Basic

Hick–Hyman Law, 1952:

```
RT = a + b · log2(n + 1)
```

**RT** is reaction time, **n** the number of alternatives. Because the growth is logarithmic, going from 2 to 4 options costs about as much as going from 4 to 8 — not double.

## Deeper

### The conditions people forget

The law was derived under specific conditions: **simple, equally probable choices, with a trained user reacting**. Real interfaces violate all three, which changes the practical advice:

- **Options are not equally likely.** If one option is chosen 80% of the time, users do not scan all of them. Order and emphasis matter more than count.
- **Categorised options behave differently.** Users perform a two-stage search — pick the category, then the item within it. This is why a 100-item menu grouped into 8 categories feels far faster than 100 flat items, even though *n* is unchanged. Hierarchy converts one big decision into two small ones.
- **Search changes the game.** Typing collapses *n* entirely. For large sets, search beats menu design.
- **Scanning ≠ deciding.** A long list of *known* items (a country picker) is a visual search problem governed by scan time, not a decision problem governed by Hick's Law.

### The common misapplication

"Hick's Law says reduce options" is used to justify removing features. Two problems: the effect is logarithmic (weak at scale), and removing capability has a cost that the law says nothing about. The honest guidance:

1. Reduce options **shown at once**, not options that exist.
2. Group and label — hierarchy beats deletion.
3. Highlight the recommended or most common path.
4. Use defaults to make the decision *pre-made but reversible*.
5. Add search when the set is large.

### Related: choice overload

Distinct from Hick's Law. Choice overload is about *satisfaction and paralysis*, not reaction time — the classic jam study found more choices attracted attention but reduced purchases. The effect is real but moderated: it bites hardest when options are similar, the user has no prior preference, and the decision is consequential. For dissimilar options with a clear preference, more choice is fine.

## Applications

- **Onboarding wizards** — one decision per screen instead of twelve on one page. Same total decisions, lower perceived load, better error recovery.
- **Restaurant-style pricing pages** — three tiers with one marked "Most popular," which pre-sorts the decision.
- **Command palettes** — collapse an enormous option space into a typed search.
- **Progressive disclosure of filters** — show 5 common filters, "More filters" for the other 20.

**Do this / not that**

- *Not that:* A flat "select your country" dropdown of 195 items sorted alphabetically.
- *Do this:* A typeahead field with the user's likely country pre-detected and the top few pinned, still allowing full browsing.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "This navigation has 14 items. What do you do?"
- "State Hick's Law." (Screening.)
- "Is fewer options always better?"

**The strong answer to "is fewer always better?"** — "No. The relationship is logarithmic, so the marginal cost of each extra option shrinks, and the law assumes equally likely choices, which is rarely true. What actually helps is structure: grouping, ordering by likelihood, defaults, and search. I would remove options only when I have data that they are unused, because removal trades a small speed gain for lost capability and a support cost."

**For the 14-item navigation**, do not jump to "cut to 7." Say: "First I would check usage — which items carry traffic, and what tasks are people trying to do. Then group into 4–6 labelled categories validated with a card sort and a tree test, promote the top tasks, and consider search. Cutting to a magic number without data usually just hides work rather than removing it."
