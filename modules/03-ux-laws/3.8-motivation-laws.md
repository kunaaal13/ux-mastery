# 3.8 Motivation Laws — Zeigarnik, Goal-Gradient, Parkinson

Three laws about *drive*: what makes people continue, accelerate, or expand a task.

---

## Zeigarnik Effect

### In one line

Unfinished tasks stay in mind more than completed ones.

### Basic

Bluma Zeigarnik, 1927, after noticing waiters recalled unpaid orders in detail and forgot them once settled. Incomplete tasks maintain a kind of cognitive tension.

### Deeper

Interface uses:
- **Progress indicators on incomplete profiles** — "Your profile is 60% complete" creates and then exploits the tension.
- **Checklists with unticked items** — onboarding checklists work on this mechanism.
- **Saved drafts and "continue where you left off"** — makes resumption cheap so the tension can be resolved.
- **Serialised content** — cliffhangers, "next episode" queues.

**The failure modes:**
- Manufactured incompleteness ("your profile is 80% complete" when the remaining 20% is optional marketing data) reads as manipulation and, once noticed, damages trust.
- Permanent badges and counts that can never reach zero create ambient anxiety rather than motivation.
- Multiple simultaneous incomplete tasks produce stress, not drive.

The ethical version: **use it to help users finish things they wanted to finish.**

---

## Goal-Gradient Effect

### In one line

Motivation increases as you get closer to a goal — and *perceived* progress works as well as real progress.

### Basic

Clark Hull, 1930s (rats ran faster near the end of a maze). The famous consumer study: loyalty cards requiring 10 stamps but issued with 2 already stamped were completed faster than cards requiring 8 from empty — identical remaining work, higher completion, because artificial advancement moved people onto the steep part of the gradient.

### Deeper

Applications:
- **Progress bars in multi-step flows.** Show progress, and consider starting above zero if the user has genuinely already done something (created an account, verified an email).
- **Front-load the easy steps.** Early wins accelerate the gradient. Ask cheap questions first.
- **Show remaining, not just completed** — "2 steps left" is more motivating than "step 3 of 5" for the same state, because it foregrounds nearness.
- **Chunk long tasks into visible milestones** so there is always a near goal.

**Trade-offs:** front-loading easy questions raises completion but can lower data quality; endowed progress crosses into deception if the "given" progress corresponds to nothing.

**Interaction with sunk cost.** The same gradient that helps users complete a form they want also traps users in flows they should abandon. If your progress bar is keeping people in a purchase they will regret, you are on the wrong side of the line.

---

## Parkinson's Law

### In one line

Work expands to fill the time available for its completion.

### Basic

Cyril Northcote Parkinson, 1955, about bureaucracy. In UX it appears in two ways:

1. **User-facing:** if you give a user unlimited time, the task takes longer. Constraints create momentum.
2. **Team-facing:** design work expands to fill the sprint. Timeboxing exploration is a real design skill.

### Deeper

User-facing applications:
- **Countdown timers on carts and bookings** — legitimate when inventory is genuinely reserved, a dark pattern when the number is fabricated or resets on reload.
- **Autofill and defaults** — reduce the available surface for deliberation, which shortens the task.
- **Deadlines and reminders** in productivity tools — externalise the constraint the user chose.

Team-facing applications:
- Timebox divergence explicitly ("6 concepts by Thursday, decision Friday").
- Prefer a shipped-and-measured B over an unshipped perfect A.

The honest caution: artificial urgency is the most common dark pattern in commerce. Ask whether the constraint is real. If the timer resets when the user reloads, it was never a constraint — it was a lie with a countdown.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "How would you increase completion of our onboarding?"
- "Is a countdown timer a dark pattern?"

**For onboarding completion**, chain the laws with trade-offs: "I would show progress and start it above zero if the user has genuinely already done something — the goal-gradient effect makes nearness motivating. I would sequence easy, low-risk questions first to get onto the steep part of the gradient, and defer anything that requires the user to fetch a document. A visible checklist uses the Zeigarnik effect to keep the unfinished task salient, and save-and-resume lets them resolve it later rather than abandoning. The trade-off is data quality — front-loading easy fields can produce rushed answers on the later ones — so I would check field-level error rates after the change, not just completion."

**For the timer question**, give the test: "Is the constraint real, is it accurately described, and does it survive a page reload? A held inventory slot with a real expiry is information. A fabricated countdown is coercion, and it is increasingly a regulatory problem, not only an ethical one."
