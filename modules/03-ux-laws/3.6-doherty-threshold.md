# 3.6 The Doherty Threshold

## In one line

Below about **400 ms** of system response, attention holds and productivity rises sharply — above it, the user's mind drifts and the interaction feels like waiting.

## Basic

Walter Doherty and Arvind Thadhani, IBM, 1982. They found that when computer response dropped below 400 ms, user productivity increased dramatically — more than enough to justify the hardware cost. The threshold is where the interaction stops feeling like a conversation and starts feeling like a queue.

Related human-factors limits (Nielsen's response-time triad, from Miller 1968):

| Duration | Perception | Design response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **< 100 ms** | Feels instantaneous, direct manipulation | No feedback needed |
| **~ 100–400 ms** | Feels responsive; attention holds | Immediate state change is enough |
| **~ 1 s** | Flow of thought maintained, but noticeable | Show a state change or subtle indicator |
| **~ 1–10 s** | Attention wanders | Determinate progress indicator, keep the user informed |
| **> 10 s** | User switches to another task | Give an estimate, allow background completion, notify on finish |

## Deeper

### Perceived performance is a design variable

You cannot always make the system faster, but you can nearly always make it *feel* faster:

- **Optimistic UI** — show the result immediately, reconcile with the server after. A sent message appears instantly; a failure is handled as an exception. Requires a clear failure path, or you have lied to the user.
- **Skeleton screens** — outline the incoming layout rather than showing a spinner. They reduce perceived wait because the page appears to be assembling, and they prevent layout shift.
- **Progressive rendering** — show what is ready (text first, images after) rather than blocking on everything.
- **Preloading and prefetching** — fetch the likely next screen during the user's decision time, which is free time.
- **Occupied time feels shorter than unoccupied time** — a well-designed wait with informative steps ("Checking availability… Confirming seats…") feels shorter than a silent one of the same duration.
- **The end of the wait dominates memory** — a fast finish is remembered better than a fast start (Peak–End, `3.7`).

### The counter-intuitive case: making things slower

Sometimes an *instant* result reduces trust. Insurance-quote and background-check products have found that adding an artificial delay with visible "work" ("Comparing 42 providers…") increased perceived value and conversion, because instantaneous results felt like the system had not really looked. Labour illusion is real — and it is on the ethical borderline: acceptable when work genuinely happens and you are pacing its disclosure, manipulative when nothing is happening.

### Spinners vs. progress bars

- **< 1 s:** nothing, or a subtle inline state.
- **1–4 s:** indeterminate spinner is acceptable.
- **> 4 s:** determinate progress with meaningful stages; an indeterminate spinner at 30 seconds is indistinguishable from a hang.
- **Unknown duration:** show *what* is happening, not a fake percentage that stalls at 99%.

### Latency and errors

Slow systems create their own usability problems: double submissions, rage clicks, abandoned flows, and users who conclude the button is broken. Disable-on-submit, idempotency, and immediate acknowledgement of the tap are latency-driven interaction requirements.

## Applications

- **Search-as-you-type** — results within ~100 ms keep the interaction conversational; above ~400 ms, users stop typing to wait, which destroys the pattern.
- **Instagram-style optimistic likes** — the heart fills instantly regardless of network round-trip.
- **Checkout spinners with stage labels** — "Authorising payment" then "Confirming order" makes a 6-second wait legible instead of anxious.

**Do this / not that**

- *Not that:* A full-page blocking spinner while a single widget loads.
- *Do this:* Render the page, skeleton only the widget, and keep the rest interactive.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "Our app is slow and we cannot fix the backend this quarter. What can design do?"
- "How do you handle loading states?"

**Structure for the slow-backend question:**
1. **Measure where it hurts** — not average latency, but latency at the moments users are waiting on a decision. p75/p95, not the mean.
2. **Remove the wait from the critical path** — optimistic UI, background processing with notification, prefetch during decision time.
3. **Make the remaining wait legible** — skeletons, staged progress, honest estimates.
4. **Protect against latency-caused errors** — disable on submit, idempotent requests, preserve form state.
5. **Then name the limit** — "None of this fixes a 30-second operation. Past about 10 seconds the right pattern is to let the user leave and notify them, not to make the spinner nicer."

Naming step 5 is what makes it a senior answer.
