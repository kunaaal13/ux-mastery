# 3.9 Aesthetics & Simplicity — Aesthetic–Usability, Occam, Pareto, Prägnanz

---

## Aesthetic–Usability Effect

### In one line

People perceive attractive designs as more usable — and forgive their flaws more readily.

### Basic

Masaaki Kurosu and Kaori Kashimura (Hitachi, 1995) tested ATM layouts and found perceived ease of use correlated more strongly with aesthetic appeal than with actual usability. Replicated many times since.

### Deeper

Two real consequences:

1. **Aesthetics buy tolerance, not correctness.** An attractive product gets more patience for minor friction, more benefit of the doubt, and better satisfaction scores at equal task performance. This is a genuine business asset.
2. **Aesthetics mask problems in testing.** Users of a beautiful prototype report fewer issues than the behaviour data shows. If you rely on self-report from a polished prototype, you will under-count usability problems.

Practical implications:
- Test *behaviour* (task success, time, errors, hesitation), not only satisfaction, when the prototype is attractive.
- Consider testing structure at low fidelity precisely so aesthetics do not suppress criticism.
- Do not use the effect to justify skipping usability work: tolerance has limits, and it collapses when a user loses money, data, or time.
- Credibility is part of this: for financial, medical, and commerce products, visual quality is a **trust signal**, and low visual quality directly suppresses conversion regardless of usability.

**Do this / not that**
- *Not that:* "The redesign tested better" — based on preference ratings alone.
- *Do this:* Report task success and time alongside preference, and note when the two diverge. Divergence is a finding, not noise.

## Occam's Razor

### In one line

Among competing solutions that work equally well, choose the one with the fewest assumptions and moving parts.

### Deeper

In design practice this is a tie-breaker, not a mandate to strip. Its correct use:
- Between two designs with equal predicted outcomes, take the simpler one — fewer states to design, fewer bugs, fewer ways to confuse.
- Remove elements that do not serve the primary or a necessary secondary task.
- Prefer removing a decision to explaining it.

Its incorrect use: "simplify" as a justification for removing capability users depend on. See Tesler (`3.5`) — the complexity does not vanish. The rigorous version of the razor asks *what evidence supports each element existing*, and removes those with none, rather than removing whatever looks busy.

A useful checklist for each element on a screen: does it enable an action, communicate state, provide required legal/trust information, or aid comprehension? If none, it is decoration — which can still be justified for brand, but should be a deliberate choice, not an accident.

## Pareto Principle (80/20)

### In one line

Roughly 80% of effects come from 20% of causes: a minority of features drive most of the usage, and a minority of problems cause most of the pain.

### Deeper

Uses:
- **Prioritisation.** Optimise the top tasks ruthlessly; make the long tail merely possible. A CRM where 5 fields are used constantly and 40 rarely should not lay them out as equals.
- **Bug and research triage.** A small number of usability issues typically account for most of the failed tasks — fix by severity × frequency, not by count.
- **The long tail caveat.** In aggregate the remaining 20% of usage may matter enormously — accessibility needs, admin workflows, and enterprise edge cases often live there. "Only 5% use it" is not the same as "it does not matter," especially if that 5% is the buyer or the regulator.

The honest phrasing for interviews: "I use it to sequence, not to exclude."

## Law of Prägnanz (Simplicity / Good Figure)

### In one line

The eye interprets ambiguous or complex images in the simplest form available.

### Deeper

This is the umbrella Gestalt principle (Module 04 covers the family in detail). Two design implications:

- **Simple forms are processed faster and remembered better** — hence the historical drift of logos and icons toward geometric simplicity.
- **Ambiguity resolves toward the simplest reading, which may not be your intended one.** If a layout can be read as two columns *or* as four groups, users will take the simpler reading. Spacing and alignment decide which reading wins (see the Law of Proximity).

Practical test: squint at your screen. What groups survive? Those are the groups users perceive, regardless of what your component tree says.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "Does visual design actually matter?" (Aesthetic–Usability.)
- "How do you decide what to cut?"
- "How do you prioritise which usability issues to fix?"

**For "does visual design matter?"** — "Yes, and for two measurable reasons: users judge attractive interfaces as more usable and tolerate small friction better, and for anything involving money or personal data, visual quality functions as a trust signal that shows up directly in conversion. The caveat I hold onto is that aesthetics suppress *reported* problems, so I make sure I am reading task success and error rates, not just satisfaction scores, when the prototype looks finished."

**For prioritisation** — give the formula: severity × frequency × (impact on the top task), then note the Pareto caveat about the long tail containing accessibility and enterprise needs.
