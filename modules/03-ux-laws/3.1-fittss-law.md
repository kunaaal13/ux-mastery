# 3.1 Fitts's Law

## In one line

The time to acquire a target is a function of the distance to it and its size: far and small is slow, near and large is fast.

## Basic

Paul Fitts, 1954, studying human motor movement. The formula:

```
MT = a + b · log2(2D / W)
```

- **MT** — movement time
- **D** — distance from the starting point to the target centre
- **W** — width of the target along the axis of motion
- **a, b** — empirical constants of the device/limb

The bracketed term is the **Index of Difficulty**. Two levers only: **make it bigger, or make it closer.**

**Everyday analogy.** Hitting a dartboard bullseye from across the room versus tapping a dinner plate on the table in front of you.

## Deeper

### Consequences that are not obvious

- **Screen edges are infinitely large targets.** The pointer stops at the edge, so a target pinned to the edge effectively has infinite width along that axis. This is why the macOS menu bar (top edge) is faster to hit than a Windows in-window menu, and why the corners are the four fastest pixels on a screen — infinite in two dimensions.
- **Distance is measured from where the cursor *is*,** not from the centre of the screen. Contextual menus appear at the cursor for exactly this reason, and so should confirmation actions after an interaction.
- **W is measured along the direction of travel.** A wide, short button approached from above is functionally a thin target. This is why long thin toolbars can be slow.
- **Touch is not mouse.** For touch, the constraints are finger size and occlusion. Recommended minimum touch targets: **44×44 pt (Apple HIG)**, **48×48 dp (Material, with 8 dp spacing)**. WCAG 2.2 sets a Target Size (Minimum) success criterion of **24×24 CSS px** at AA and 44×44 at AAA.
- **Thumb zones.** On phones, reachability matters more than raw distance: the bottom third of a large phone is the comfortable zone for one-handed use, the top corners are the worst. This is why primary actions and tab bars migrated to the bottom of mobile interfaces.

### The dangerous corollary

Fitts's Law makes destructive actions *easier* too. A large "Delete" adjacent to a large "Save" is fast to hit — including by accident. Deliberate anti-Fitts design is legitimate: separate destructive actions, make them smaller or further away, or require a second gesture. The rule is not "make everything big," it is **make things as easy to hit as their consequences deserve.**

### Related: Steering Law

For navigating *through* a constrained path (a nested dropdown corridor), time depends on the path's length and width, not just the endpoint. This is why cascading menus that require travelling diagonally through a narrow strip are frustrating, and why good implementations use a forgiving triangle of tolerance toward the submenu.

## Applications

- **Bottom navigation bars on mobile** — short thumb travel, edge-anchored.
- **Right-click / long-press context menus** — zero travel distance.
- **The Mac Dock and full-width top menu bar** — edge exploitation.
- **A "Save" button that follows a long form** — placed where the eye and cursor already are, not in a distant header.
- **Hover targets sized to the row, not the text** — clicking a list item should not require hitting 40 px of link text.

**Do this / not that**

- *Not that:* A 20×20 px close icon in a corner with 2 px padding.
- *Do this:* A 44×44 pt hit area with a 20 px visual icon centred inside it. Visual size and hit size are independent — expand the hit area without changing the design.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "Why do mobile apps put navigation at the bottom?"
- "What is Fitts's Law and where have you applied it?"
- "How would you speed up this toolbar?"

**How to answer well.** State the law in one sentence, then *immediately* give the non-obvious consequence — edges and corners, or the hit-area-versus-visual-size distinction. Then name the counter-case: "It also argues for separating destructive actions, because the law makes mistakes faster too."

**Trap to avoid:** saying "so make buttons bigger." That is the poster version. The senior version is "increase the *hit area*, reduce travel from the user's current position, exploit edges, and deliberately shrink or isolate destructive targets."
