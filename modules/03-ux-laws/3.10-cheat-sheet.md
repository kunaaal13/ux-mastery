# 3.10 UX Laws — Cheat Sheet

One-line recall. Cover the right column and recite. Then, for each, say one application **and one counter-case** out loud — that is the interview-ready standard.

| Law | Statement | Prime application | Counter-case / limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Fitts's Law** | Time to hit a target depends on distance and size | Bottom nav, edge menus, big hit areas | Makes destructive actions easy to hit too; deliberately isolate them |
| **Steering Law** | Time through a constrained path depends on path length and width | Forgiving submenu corridors | Only applies to path traversal, not point targets |
| **Hick's Law** | Decision time grows logarithmically with number of options | Wizards, tiered pricing, progressive filters | Logarithmic = weak; grouping and defaults beat deletion |
| **Miller's Law** | ~7±2 (realistically ~4) chunks in working memory | Chunked card numbers, sticky headers | Not about menu length; visible items are not memory |
| **Jakob's Law** | Users expect your site to work like the others they know | Standard nav, cart top-right, familiar icons | Conventions can be collectively bad (hamburger menus) |
| **Tesler's Law** | Complexity is conserved; it can only move | Auto-detect card type, smart defaults | Hidden complexity needs an escape hatch |
| **Postel's Law** | Liberal in what you accept, conservative in what you send | Accept any phone format, trim whitespace | Do not silently guess genuinely ambiguous input |
| **Doherty Threshold** | Under ~400 ms keeps attention and productivity | Optimistic UI, skeletons, prefetch | Past ~10 s, let users leave and notify them |
| **Serial Position** | First and last items are remembered best | Key nav items at both ends | About memory, not visual attention |
| **Von Restorff** | The different item is remembered | One primary button, "Most popular" tier | Requires a plain background; overuse kills it |
| **Peak–End Rule** | Experience is judged by its peak and its ending | Fix the worst moment; design confirmations and cancellation | Duration is discounted, not irrelevant — pain still costs |
| **Zeigarnik Effect** | Unfinished tasks stay in mind | Progress meters, onboarding checklists | Manufactured incompleteness reads as manipulation |
| **Goal-Gradient** | Motivation rises near the goal; endowed progress works | Progress bars starting above zero, easy steps first | Feeds sunk cost; can trap users in flows they should quit |
| **Parkinson's Law** | Work expands to fill available time | Real deadlines, timeboxed exploration | Fake countdowns are a dark pattern |
| **Aesthetic–Usability** | Attractive designs are perceived as more usable | Visual quality as a trust signal | Suppresses *reported* problems — measure behaviour |
| **Occam's Razor** | Prefer the solution with fewest assumptions | Tie-breaker between equal designs | Not a licence to delete needed capability |
| **Pareto (80/20)** | A minority of causes drive most effects | Optimise top tasks, triage by severity × frequency | The tail holds accessibility and enterprise needs |
| **Prägnanz** | The eye takes the simplest available interpretation | Squint test for grouping | The simplest reading may not be your intended one |

## The 90-second drill

Set a timer. Pick three laws at random. For each, say:

1. The statement (1 sentence)
2. One product you can point to (1 sentence)
3. One case where it misleads (1 sentence)

If you cannot do 3, you do not know the law yet — you know its name.

## The five laws to know cold if you only have one hour

Fitts, Hick, Jakob, Doherty, Peak–End. They cover motor cost, decision cost, expectation, time perception, and memory — which is most of what an interviewer will probe.
