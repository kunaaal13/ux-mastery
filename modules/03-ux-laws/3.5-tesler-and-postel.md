# 3.5 Tesler's Law & Postel's Law

## Tesler's Law — the Conservation of Complexity

### In one line

Every system has an irreducible amount of complexity; the only question is **who absorbs it** — the user, the designer, or the engineering.

### Basic

Larry Tesler (of Xerox PARC, and the man behind cut/copy/paste) argued that once a system is as simple as it can be, remaining complexity cannot be removed — it can only be moved. If the interface hides it, someone else is carrying it.

**Everyday analogy.** An automatic gearbox does not remove gear-changing. It moves the work from the driver to the machine. Someone still designed and built that transmission, and it is more complex than a manual.

### Deeper

Three places complexity can live:

1. **The user.** Cheapest to build, most expensive at scale. Every user pays the cost, every time.
2. **The design.** Smart defaults, inference, progressive disclosure, good information architecture. Costs designer effort once.
3. **The engineering.** Automatic detection, sync, background reconciliation, migration logic. Costs the most to build and maintain — and creates its own failure modes.

The decision rule: **push complexity toward whoever pays for it least often.** A step performed by ten million users daily deserves substantial engineering complexity. A step performed by an admin twice a year does not.

The trap in the other direction: pushing complexity into the system creates *invisible* complexity, which fails in ways users cannot diagnose. Autocorrect, auto-formatting, and aggressive smart defaults are complexity absorbed by the system that occasionally hands the user a much harder problem — "why did it do that, and how do I undo it?" Absorbed complexity needs an **escape hatch**.

### Applications

- **Gmail's spam filtering** — enormous engineering complexity absorbed so that users do not triage. Escape hatch: the Spam folder.
- **Card-number auto-detection** — the system infers the card type rather than asking. Trivial engineering, removes a decision from every user.
- **Timezones** — someone must decide what "9am" means. Either the user specifies, or the system infers and displays the assumption. The one thing you cannot do is pretend the complexity is absent.

**Do this / not that**
- *Not that:* Ask the user to pick an image size, format, and compression level before upload.
- *Do this:* Handle it automatically, show what you did, and expose an "advanced" override for the rare user who needs it.

### Interview angle

Asked as: "How do you simplify a complex product?" A weak answer says "remove features." A strong answer: "I start by finding where the complexity is irreducible — the domain genuinely has these states — and then decide who should carry each piece. Anything performed by many users frequently should be absorbed by the system or resolved by a default. Anything rare and expert-only can stay explicit. And absorbed complexity always needs a visible trace and an escape hatch, or it becomes unexplainable behaviour."

## Postel's Law — the Robustness Principle

### In one line

Be liberal in what you accept, and conservative in what you send.

### Basic

Originally a networking principle (Jon Postel, TCP). In UX it means: **accept input in whatever reasonable form the user provides, and output in a consistent, predictable form.**

Users type `+91 98765 43210`, `09876543210`, and `98765-43210`. All three are the same number. Rejecting two of them is the design being lazy at the user's expense.

### Deeper

Applications of liberal acceptance:

- Strip spaces, dashes, and parentheses from phone and card numbers rather than rejecting them
- Accept multiple date formats and echo back the interpreted date so the user can verify
- Trim leading/trailing whitespace on emails and codes (a large share of "invalid code" errors are pasted whitespace)
- Case-insensitive matching for codes, emails, and usernames
- Accept a pasted full URL where a handle is expected

Conservative output means: consistent formatting, predictable behaviour, clear system state, no surprising side effects.

**The limits.** Liberal acceptance must not create ambiguity. `03/04/2026` is a real conflict between conventions; guessing silently is worse than asking. The rule: **accept freely where interpretation is unambiguous; where it is ambiguous, interpret and show your interpretation** rather than either rejecting or guessing silently.

Robustness also covers tolerance for human variability: fat-finger taps near a target, double submissions, slow or interrupted connections, and the back button. Designing for the messy real case is not edge-case work; it is most of the work.

### Applications

- **Forgiving format on payment fields** — measurably reduces form errors.
- **Undo instead of confirm** — accepting an imperfect action and offering reversal, rather than interrogating every action up front. Undo is often a better safeguard than a confirmation dialog, which users learn to click through.
- **Idempotent submissions** — a double-tapped "Pay" must not charge twice. This is a UX requirement expressed as an engineering one.

**Do this / not that**
- *Not that:* "Invalid phone number. Please enter digits only, no spaces."
- *Do this:* Accept it, normalise it, display the normalised version, and only error when the number genuinely cannot be a phone number.

### Interview angle

Asked as: "How do you design error handling?" or "How would you reduce form errors?" Answer in a priority order: **prevent** (constraints, input masks, good defaults), **accept** (liberal parsing, normalise and echo), **catch early** (inline validation on blur, not on submit), **explain** (say what is wrong and how to fix it, in the user's words, next to the field), **recover** (preserve entered data, offer undo, never clear the form).

That ordered list is a reusable answer for a whole family of questions.
