# 3.7 Memory Laws — Serial Position, Von Restorff, Peak–End

Three laws about what people *remember*, which is different from what they experienced.

---

## Serial Position Effect

### In one line

Items at the **beginning** and **end** of a sequence are remembered best; the middle is where information goes to die.

### Basic

From Ebbinghaus. Two components:
- **Primacy** — early items get more rehearsal and reach long-term memory.
- **Recency** — recent items are still in working memory.

### Deeper

Design consequences:
- Put the most important navigation items **first and last**. This is why "Home" sits at the left edge and "Sign up"/"Cart"/"Account" sits at the right edge of a nav bar, with less critical items buried in the middle.
- In mobile bottom tabs, the outer positions carry the most weight — and, combined with Fitts's Law and thumb reach, the far corners are contested space.
- In a list of options where you want free recall later (a pricing comparison, a feature list), lead and close with the strongest items.
- **Counter-use:** knowing the middle is weak also tells you where *not* to hide things you are ethically obliged to make visible. Burying a fee in the middle of a list is a dark pattern with a psychological mechanism behind it.

The effect concerns *memory*, not visual attention. For scanning, position matters through reading order and pre-attentive salience instead.

## Von Restorff Effect (Isolation Effect)

### In one line

When several similar items are present, the one that differs is the one remembered.

### Basic

Hedwig von Restorff, 1933. Difference is what encodes.

### Deeper

- The effect requires **similarity in the surrounding set**. Emphasis is relative: one highlighted plan among three plain ones pops; three highlighted plans among three pop nothing.
- Difference can be created with any pre-attentive attribute (Module 02): colour, size, weight, enclosure, position, motion.
- **Never encode with colour alone.** Users with colour-vision deficiency, greyscale printing, and low-contrast environments all break that encoding. Pair colour with a badge, a label, an outline, or size (Module 10).
- **Overuse destroys it.** Every "NEW!" badge devalues every other badge. Emphasis is a budget: spend it once per view.

Applications: "Most popular" pricing tier, a single filled primary button per screen, a destructive action styled distinctly, unread indicators in a list.

**Do this / not that**
- *Not that:* Highlighting the recommended plan with colour, a badge, a scale transform, a border, and a shadow, while also badging two other plans.
- *Do this:* One clear difference on one plan; leave the rest plain.

## Peak–End Rule

### In one line

People judge an experience mostly by its most intense moment and its ending, not by the sum or average of the whole.

### Basic

Kahneman and colleagues. In the classic study, participants rated a longer painful experience with a milder ending as *better* than a shorter one that ended at peak pain — even though the longer one contained strictly more discomfort. Duration is heavily discounted in memory ("duration neglect"); peaks and endings dominate.

### Deeper

Design consequences:

- **Find and fix the negative peak.** One catastrophic moment — a lost form, a rejected payment with no explanation, a support dead end — dominates the memory of an otherwise good product. Locating the worst moment in a journey is higher leverage than raising the average.
- **Design the ending deliberately.** Endings include: order confirmation, task completion, session close, subscription cancellation, and error resolution. Most products invest heavily in onboarding (a beginning) and neglect every ending.
- **Create a positive peak.** A moment of delight that lands *at the point of user relief or accomplishment* is remembered; decoration sprinkled everywhere is not.
- **Cancellation is an ending.** A graceful cancellation flow is the last thing a user remembers, and it heavily influences whether they return or recommend. Obstructive cancellation buys a month of revenue and sells a permanent negative peak.

Applications: Mailchimp's send-confirmation moment, Duolingo's lesson-complete animation, a delivery app's "arrived" screen, a well-written empty-state after a user clears their inbox.

**Do this / not that**
- *Not that:* A confirmation page that says "Success" in grey text and nothing else, after a 12-step application.
- *Do this:* Confirm what happened, what happens next, when, and give one clear next action — plus a small moment of warmth proportionate to the effort spent.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "Where would you put the most important navigation items and why?"
- "How would you make our product feel more delightful?" (Peak–End is the disciplined answer.)
- "Our NPS is fine but users describe us negatively. What would you look at?"

**How to answer the delight question well.** Weak answers propose animations. Strong answer: "Delight is cheapest and most durable when it is applied at the peak and the end rather than spread everywhere. So first I would map the journey and find the emotional low — usually an error, a wait, or a dead end — because removing a negative peak moves memory more than adding a positive one. Then I would invest in endings, which most products under-design: confirmations, completions, and cancellation. And I would keep the emphasis budget tight so that the moments that are special stay special — Von Restorff only works against a plain background."

That answer chains three laws and names a trade-off. It is a strong 90-second response.
