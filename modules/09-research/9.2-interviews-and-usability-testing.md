# 9.2 Interviews & Usability Testing

## Part 1 — User interviews

### The goal

Understand behaviour, context, and motivation — not to collect feature requests. Users are experts on their problems and unreliable designers of solutions.

### Rules for good questions

- **Ask about the past, not the future.** "Tell me about the last time you…" beats "would you use…" — retrospective accounts of concrete events are far more reliable than predictions.
- **Ask for stories, not summaries.** "Walk me through what happened" beats "what do you usually do."
- **Never ask leading questions.** "How frustrating was that?" plants the answer. Ask "what was that like?"
- **Avoid hypotheticals.** "Would you pay for this?" reliably overestimates willingness.
- **Ask why up to about three times**, then stop — repeated interrogation makes people invent reasons.
- **Embrace silence.** The most useful sentences arrive after an uncomfortable pause.
- **Ask about workarounds.** "Show me how you handle that today" surfaces spreadsheets, sticky notes, and WhatsApp threads — the highest-value findings in most B2B research.

### Structure

1. **Warm-up** — build rapport, set expectations, get consent for recording.
2. **Context** — role, situation, what a typical period looks like.
3. **Recent concrete episodes** — the core of the session.
4. **Probes on the episodes** — why, what happened next, what was hard.
5. **Wrap** — anything I should have asked; snowball referrals.

Keep to 45–60 minutes. Take notes on verbatims, not paraphrases; the user's exact words are what you will use for labels and copy later.

### Contextual inquiry

Interviewing *in the environment* while watching real work — a master/apprentice relationship. Expensive, and the highest-yield generative method for professional tools, because people cannot describe the parts of their work they have automated.

## Part 2 — Usability testing

### Setup

- **Tasks, not questions.** "Buy a pair of running shoes under ₹3,000 for delivery this week" — realistic, specific, with a defined end state, and not phrased using the interface's own labels (that turns it into a word-matching exercise).
- **Think-aloud protocol.** Ask participants to narrate. It reveals expectation gaps. Caveat: it slows people down and can change behaviour, so avoid it for timing measurements — use retrospective think-aloud there.
- **5–8 participants per segment**, iterating between rounds. Two rounds of 5 beat one round of 10.
- **Prototype fidelity** matched to the question (Module 01).

### Moderating

- **Say nothing while they struggle.** Silence is data. Intervene only when the session is otherwise unusable.
- **Bounce questions back:** "What would you expect to happen?" not "It's the button at the top."
- **Never defend the design.** The moment you explain, you have stopped learning.
- **Watch behaviour over commentary.** People will say "that was easy" after failing; write down what they did.
- **Ask post-task, not mid-task:** SEQ ("Overall, how difficult or easy was that task?") after each task gives a cheap comparable number.

### Moderated vs. unmoderated

| | Moderated | Unmoderated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Depth | High — you can probe | Low — no follow-up |
| Scale/cost | Slower, costlier | Fast, cheap, larger n |
| Best for | Complex flows, early designs, understanding *why* | Validating known flows, benchmarks, wide coverage |
| Risk | Moderator bias | Misunderstood tasks, no recovery from confusion |

### What to record

Per task: success (binary or graded), time, errors, points of hesitation, requests for help, quotes, and severity of each observed problem. Analyse by problem, not by participant — the aggregated problem list with severity is the deliverable.

### Remote testing notes

Remote is now the default for cost and geography. Watch for: the participant's setup (screen size and assistive technology are part of the finding, not noise), connection quality affecting perceived performance, and the fact that home context differs from the real usage context — which for many products is a bus, a shop floor, or a hospital ward.

## Common mistakes

- Testing with colleagues, friends, or anyone who knows the product.
- Writing tasks using the interface's own words.
- Demoing instead of testing — talking the participant through the flow.
- Treating preference ("I like the blue one") as usability evidence.
- Stopping after one round, so nothing is validated after the fixes.
- Ignoring the recruitment gap: no participants using assistive technology, no low-bandwidth users, no non-native speakers.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "How do you run a usability test?"
- "A stakeholder says 'just ask users what they want.' How do you respond?"
- "How do you avoid bias in research?"

**For the stakeholder question:** agree with the intent, correct the method. "Absolutely worth talking to users — I would just ask them about what they *did* rather than what they want, because people predict their own behaviour badly and tend to request faster horses. So I would ask for concrete recent episodes and look for the workarounds they have already built, which usually points at the real need more reliably than a feature request."

**For bias:** name specific mechanisms and specific mitigations — leading questions (write the guide in advance and have someone else review it), confirmation bias (write down what would disprove your hypothesis before you start; have two people synthesise independently), sampling bias (screen on behaviour, and check who is missing), moderator bias (silence, bounce-back questions, avoid defending), and social desirability (make it explicit that the design is being tested, not the person).
