# 9.4 Synthesis & Influence

## In one line

Research that does not change a decision was a hobby — synthesis and communication are where the value is created, not the sessions.

## From notes to insight

**Levels of a finding:**

1. **Observation** — "Three of eight participants scrolled past the filter bar."
2. **Pattern** — "Users did not perceive the filter bar as interactive."
3. **Insight** — "Users assume filtering lives behind a control they must open, so an always-visible bar reads as a heading."
4. **Recommendation** — "Give filters an explicit control affordance and show active filters as chips."
5. **Decision** — what the team will actually do, and how success will be judged.

Most reports stop at 2 and get ignored. Most junior designers jump to 4 without 3, so the recommendation cannot be evaluated. **The insight is the unit of value** — it is transferable to problems the study did not cover.

## Synthesis methods

- **Affinity mapping** — put every observation on a note, cluster by similarity, name the clusters. Best done with the team, because participation is what creates belief.
- **Rainbow spreadsheet** — participants as columns, findings as rows, colour per participant; frequency becomes visible instantly and it is far faster than a wall of notes for evaluative studies.
- **Journey mapping** the findings — locates problems in time and shows the emotional low (Module 01.3).
- **Severity rating** — apply the 0–4 scale (Module 05) so the list is prioritised, not just long.
- **Jobs-to-be-done statements** — for generative work, express findings as situations and desired progress.

**Guard against confirmation bias:** write down what you expect to find *before* synthesis; have two people cluster independently and compare; explicitly hunt for disconfirming cases; and count how many participants support each theme rather than relying on the most vivid quote.

## Reporting

The format that gets acted on:

1. **One-page summary** — the three to five things that matter, with recommendations and severity. Assume this is all most people read.
2. **Findings** — each with: what was observed, how many participants, evidence (clip or quote), severity, and recommendation.
3. **Method and limitations** — who was studied, what was not covered, what the study cannot claim.
4. **Appendix** — everything else.

Rules: lead with the decision the reader must make; use clips over transcripts (30 seconds of a user failing outperforms any slide); quantify qualitative findings carefully ("6 of 8" not "most users", and never a percentage from a sample of eight); and never bury a severe finding for political comfort.

## Influence

Findings do not implement themselves.

- **Involve stakeholders in the sessions.** A PM who watched two users fail needs no persuading. Observation is the cheapest influence mechanism that exists — use it deliberately, with a note-taking template so observers record behaviour rather than opinions.
- **Frame in the stakeholder's currency.** Engineering: cost and complexity. PM: metric impact and risk. Executive: revenue, retention, and support cost. The same finding, three framings.
- **Pair every problem with an option set,** including a cheap partial fix. "The right fix is a restructure; the cheap fix this sprint is relabelling two items, which addresses about half the failures" gets acted on; a purist recommendation gets deferred.
- **Make it hard to ignore:** a severity-rated list with owners, a clip reel, and a follow-up date.
- **Track what happened.** Keep a record of which findings were adopted and what the outcome was; this is how a research practice earns budget.

## When you are overruled

You will be. The professional move: state the risk once, clearly, with the evidence and the expected cost; propose a way to detect if the risk materialises (a metric, a threshold, a review date); commit to the decision; and follow up when the data arrives, without triumphalism. Being right later is worth nothing if you burned the relationship being right earlier.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "How do you synthesise research?"
- "Tell me about a time your research changed a decision."
- "What do you do when stakeholders ignore your findings?"

**For the last question**, avoid a story where you are the wronged expert. Strong answer: "Usually being ignored means I communicated in my currency rather than theirs, or I arrived after the decision was made. So I would first check the timing — research that lands after commitment is expensive to act on. Then I would reframe: for a PM, the finding becomes a risk to the activation metric with an estimated size; for engineering, a cheap partial fix alongside the full one. And I would get them in the room for the next round of sessions, because watching two users fail moves people in a way a deck never does. If it is still a no, I state the risk once, agree a metric that would tell us if it bites, and commit."

## Check yourself

1. Name the five levels from observation to decision, and which one most reports stop at.
2. What is the rainbow spreadsheet good for?
3. Give three guards against confirmation bias in synthesis.
4. Why quantify qualitative findings as "6 of 8" rather than "75%"?
5. What is the single cheapest way to make findings stick?
