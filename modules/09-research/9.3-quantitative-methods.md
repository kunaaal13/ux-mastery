# 9.3 Quantitative Methods

## Surveys

**Good for:** measuring prevalence of attitudes, segmenting, tracking a metric over time.
**Bad for:** understanding *why*, and anything about people who did not respond.

Rules:
- **One idea per question.** "Was the checkout fast and easy?" is two questions and unanswerable if the answer differs.
- **No leading language.** "How much did you enjoy…" presumes enjoyment.
- **Balanced scales** with a labelled midpoint; keep scale direction consistent throughout.
- **Behavioural questions beat attitudinal ones.** "How many times did you do X in the last 7 days?" is more reliable than "How often do you usually…"
- **Pilot it.** Every survey has at least one question that means something different to respondents than to you.
- **Beware non-response bias.** People who answer surveys differ systematically from those who do not — usually the very happy and the very angry.

**Standard instruments:**
- **SUS** — 10 items, 0–100 score; ~68 average; comparable across products.
- **SEQ** — a single 7-point post-task item; cheap and sensitive.
- **NPS** — "how likely are you to recommend…"; widely used, statistically crude, and a poor diagnostic. Track it if the business does, but never design from it alone.
- **CSAT**, **CES (Customer Effort Score)** — transactional satisfaction and effort.

## Analytics

Behavioural, complete, and mute about causes.

- **Funnels** — where people drop. The first place to look for any conversion question.
- **Retention curves** — do people come back? Flattening curve = product-market fit signal; continuously declining = leaky product.
- **Cohorts** — compare groups by join date or by exposure to a change; essential for separating "the product changed" from "the users changed."
- **Segments** — averages hide everything; always look by platform, tenure, geography, and plan.
- **Event instrumentation** — define events *before* launch, with a naming convention and a documented schema. Retro-fitting analytics is the most common preventable research failure.

**Session recordings and heatmaps** sit between qual and quant: good for generating hypotheses (rage clicks, dead clicks, form-field abandonment), unreliable as proof.

## A/B testing

**What it is good at:** causal evidence that variant B moved a metric relative to A, for the population tested, during the test window.

Essentials:
- **One primary metric**, decided in advance, plus guardrail metrics that must not degrade (latency, error rate, support contacts, revenue per user).
- **Power calculation before launch** from baseline rate, minimum detectable effect, and desired power. Underpowered tests produce noise that reads as signal.
- **Pre-commit to duration** and run at least one full weekly cycle. Stopping when the result looks good ("peeking") massively inflates false positives.
- **Watch for novelty and primacy effects** — a change may win temporarily because it is new, or lose temporarily because it is unfamiliar (change aversion, Module 03).
- **Beware multiple comparisons** — testing 20 metrics guarantees a "significant" one by chance.
- **Statistical significance is not importance.** With enough traffic, a 0.1% lift is significant and may be worthless; state the effect size and confidence interval, not just the p-value.
- **Segment the result**, but treat post-hoc segment findings as hypotheses, not conclusions.

**When not to A/B test:** low traffic; long-horizon effects (brand, trust, retention over months); high-risk irreversible changes; and anything where the two variants are different products rather than different designs.

## Benchmarking

Repeat the same task set with the same metrics on a schedule (quarterly, or per major release): task success, time on task, error rate, SUS. It converts "the redesign feels better" into a comparable number, and it is the most persuasive artifact for design's value that most teams never build.

## Metrics hygiene

- Define every metric in writing, including edge cases, and keep one source of truth.
- Prefer **rates over counts** (counts move with traffic).
- Watch **distributions, not just means** — a bimodal time-on-task hides two populations.
- Always ask **"what would make this number go up for a bad reason?"** Time-on-task rising can mean richer engagement or worse usability; session count rising can mean people cannot finish in one go.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "How would you measure the success of this feature?"
- "The A/B test shows a lift but the qualitative feedback is negative. What do you do?"
- "What is wrong with NPS?"

**For the conflicting-evidence question** — this is a favourite because it tests judgement: "First I would check the test's validity — was it powered, did it run a full cycle, was there peeking, and are the guardrail metrics intact? Then I would look at *which* metric moved: a lift in clicks with negative sentiment often means the change is more compelling but less honest, so I would check downstream metrics — completion, refunds, support contacts, and retention — because a short-term lift that increases returns is a loss. The qual is telling me the mechanism; the quant is telling me the magnitude. If the lift survives the guardrails and the qual complaints are about unfamiliarity rather than harm, I would ship and monitor; if the complaint is about being misled, I would not ship regardless of the lift."

## Check yourself

1. Name two things surveys are bad at.
2. What is peeking, and why does it matter?
3. Give three guardrail metrics for a conversion experiment.
4. When should you not run an A/B test?
5. Why is statistical significance not the same as importance?
