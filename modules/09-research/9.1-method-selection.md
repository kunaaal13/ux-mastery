# 9.1 Choosing a Method

## In one line

Pick the method from the question, not from habit — and the single most common research failure is using a method that cannot answer the question being asked.

## The three axes

**1. Qualitative vs. quantitative**
- Qual answers **why** and **how**. Small samples, rich detail, generates hypotheses.
- Quant answers **how many** and **how much**. Large samples, comparable numbers, tests hypotheses.

**2. Attitudinal vs. behavioural**
- Attitudinal = what people **say** (interviews, surveys).
- Behavioural = what people **do** (usability tests, analytics, A/B tests).
- The gap between the two is the single most important fact in research. People misreport their behaviour, cannot predict their future behaviour, and rationalise past behaviour. **Weight behaviour over self-report whenever both are available.**

**3. Generative vs. evaluative**
- Generative (discovery) — what is the problem, whose is it, what matters?
- Evaluative — does this solution work?

## The method map

| Question | Method | Typical n |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who are our users and what are they trying to achieve? | Interviews, contextual inquiry | 5–12 per segment |
| What do people actually do all day? | Diary study, contextual inquiry | 8–20 |
| Does this design work? | Moderated usability test | 5–8 per segment |
| Where exactly do people fail, at scale? | Unmoderated usability test | 20–50 |
| How do users group and name things? | Card sort | 15–30 |
| Can users find things in this structure? | Tree test | 30–50 |
| Which of two designs performs better on a metric? | A/B test | Powered by the metric (often thousands) |
| How widespread is this attitude? | Survey | 100+ for segment comparisons |
| What do people do in the product now? | Analytics, session recordings | All users |
| Why did that metric drop? | Analytics + qual follow-up | — |
| Is our usability improving over time? | Benchmark study (task success, SUS) | 20–50, repeated |
| What do competitors do, and how well? | Competitive/heuristic review | — |

## Sample sizes, honestly

- **"Five users find 85% of problems"** (Nielsen) applies to *usability problems in a single homogeneous user group*, assuming iterative rounds. It does not mean five users tell you what to build, does not apply to distinct segments (five per segment), and does not license claims about proportions ("60% of users prefer…" from 5 people is meaningless).
- **Interviews:** run until saturation — new sessions stop producing new themes. Usually 5–12 per segment.
- **Surveys:** the required sample depends on the precision you need and how you will slice it; ask what decision the number will drive before fielding it.
- **A/B tests:** the sample is determined by baseline rate, minimum detectable effect, and power. Calculate before launch, and pre-commit to the duration.

## Method limitations to state out loud

| Method | Cannot tell you |
| --- | --- |
| Interviews | What people will actually do; accurate frequencies |
| Surveys | Why, in any depth; anything about people who did not respond |
| Usability tests | Whether anyone wants the feature; real-world context and motivation |
| Analytics | Why; the experience of people who never arrived |
| A/B tests | Why; long-term effects; anything about a change too small to power |
| Heuristic evaluation | Real user behaviour; domain-specific misunderstandings |

Saying a method's limits unprompted is one of the highest-signal moves available in a research interview.

## Triangulation

Strong conclusions come from two or more independent method types pointing the same way — analytics showing where people drop, plus interviews explaining why. When qual and quant disagree, that disagreement is usually the most interesting finding, not an error to resolve away.

## Research ops basics

- **Recruiting** is the hardest part; screener quality determines study quality. Screen on *behaviour* ("how many times did you do X last month"), never on self-described attitudes, and hide the desired answer.
- **Consent and privacy** — explicit consent for recording, clear retention limits, anonymisation, and never collecting personal data you do not need. In regulated contexts, this is a legal obligation, not a courtesy.
- **Incentives** proportionate and unconditional on saying nice things.
- **A repository** so findings survive; research nobody can find gets re-run at full cost.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "How would you research this problem?"
- "What method would you use to find out X?"
- "How many users do you need?"

**How to answer any method question:** state the *question* first, then the method, then the limitation, then what you would pair it with. "The question is whether people can complete the flow, which is behavioural and evaluative, so a moderated usability test with 5–8 per segment. It will not tell me whether anyone wants the feature or how common the problem is, so I would pair it with funnel analytics for prevalence and, if the concept itself is unproven, a fake-door or concept test first."

**"How many users?"** — never answer with a bare number. "It depends on the question: 5–8 per segment for finding usability problems iteratively; saturation for interviews, usually 5–12 per segment; a powered calculation for an A/B test; and for a survey it depends on how I intend to slice the results."
