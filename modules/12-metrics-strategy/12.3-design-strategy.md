# 12.3 Design Strategy

## In one line

Strategy is deciding what *not* to do — and the designer's version is making trade-offs explicit rather than presenting single answers.

## Prioritisation frameworks

| Framework | How | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Impact vs. effort** | 2×2 | Fast triage; hides risk and confidence |
| **RICE** | Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort | Comparable scores; the Confidence term is the honest part |
| **MoSCoW** | Must/Should/Could/Won't | Scope negotiation for a fixed deadline |
| **Kano** | Basic / Performance / Delight | Deciding what earns satisfaction vs. what merely avoids anger |
| **Opportunity solution tree** | Outcome → opportunities → solutions → experiments | Keeping work tied to an outcome rather than a feature list |

**Kano is worth knowing properly** because it explains a common confusion: *basic* expectations (the app does not crash, the search works) generate no satisfaction when present but severe dissatisfaction when absent; *performance* attributes scale linearly with satisfaction; *delight* attributes create disproportionate satisfaction but are not missed if absent — and they decay into basics over time (a fingerprint unlock was delight, then performance, now basic). Teams routinely fund delight while basics are broken.

## Making trade-offs explicit

The most valuable thing a designer brings to a prioritisation conversation is a clear statement of what each option costs:

- **Speed vs. flexibility** — a wizard is faster to learn, slower to use repeatedly.
- **Simplicity vs. capability** — every removed option costs someone.
- **Consistency vs. optimisation** — a system component may be 90% right here; using a bespoke one costs consistency and maintenance.
- **Novice vs. expert** — optimise for learnability or efficiency; you rarely get both without layering.
- **Now vs. later** — a fast fix that forecloses the right structure is sometimes correct; say so explicitly rather than pretending it is free.

Presenting two or three options at different points on a trade-off axis, with a recommendation, is the most reliably senior move available in any review.

## Arguing for design work

- **Lead with the outcome**, not the craft. "This reduces support contacts on the top ticket topic by an estimated X" beats "this is more consistent."
- **Quantify what you can, and be honest about the range.** A stated assumption is credible; a fake precise number is not.
- **Use cost-side numbers** when revenue attribution is hard (Module 12.2).
- **Bring the cheap version.** Every proposal should include the version that fits this sprint, so the answer can be "yes, partly" instead of "not now."
- **Show the risk of not doing it,** with a way to detect it — a metric and a threshold.

## Design debt

Like technical debt: shortcuts that compound. Track it explicitly — inconsistent patterns, unaddressed usability findings, accessibility gaps, unsupported states. Give each an owner and a severity. Untracked design debt is invisible to planning and therefore never funded.

## Working within constraints

Constraint is the normal condition, not the exception. The professional response:
- Ask what the constraint actually is (deadline? headcount? platform? legal?) — the stated constraint is often not the binding one.
- Solve for the constraint rather than complaining about it: what is the best experience achievable in three days?
- Name what is being traded away and log it as debt, so the decision is visible later.
- Protect the floor: accessibility blockers and data loss are not tradeable, and saying so once, clearly, is part of the job.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "How do you prioritise?"
- "How do you say no?"
- "Tell me about a trade-off you made."
- "How do you measure the value of design?"

**For "how do you say no":** "I try not to say no to a *need*, only to a solution. Usually a request arrives as a feature; I want the outcome behind it, because there is often a cheaper path to the same outcome. If it is genuinely lower value than what it displaces, I make the trade-off visible — here is what we would delay, here is the expected difference — and let the person with the priority mandate choose. Saying no is rarely mine to do alone; making the cost legible is."

**For trade-offs**, have a real story with a number and a regret. The strongest version includes what you gave up, why it was still right, and what you would watch to catch it going wrong.
