# 12.2 Product Metrics & Business Framing

## The funnel

**Acquisition → Activation → Retention → Revenue → Referral** (AARRR, "pirate metrics").

- **Acquisition** — people arrive. Mostly marketing's lever, but landing-page design matters.
- **Activation** — people reach first value. **This is design's highest-leverage stage** and the one most connected to onboarding work.
- **Retention** — they come back. The strongest indicator of product health; nothing else compensates for a leaking bucket.
- **Revenue** — they pay.
- **Referral** — they bring others.

**Activation deserves a precise definition:** the moment a user first experiences the core value ("sent a first message", "imported first dataset", "completed a first transfer"), not "finished the tour." Defining it well is often the most valuable analytical work a designer can do, because everything upstream can then be optimised toward something meaningful.

## Retention

- **Cohort retention curves** — group users by join week and track return rates. A curve that **flattens** indicates a group that keeps coming back (a product-market fit signal); a curve declining to zero means no durable value.
- **N-day vs. rolling vs. bracket retention** — define which you use and stay consistent; they produce very different numbers.
- **Resurrection** — dormant users returning; often cheaper to win than new acquisition.
- **Churn drivers** — separate voluntary (chose to leave) from involuntary (payment failed). Involuntary churn is a design and operations problem with a surprisingly large payoff.

## North-star metrics

A single metric capturing the value delivered to users, which the business believes predicts long-term growth. Good ones measure *delivered value*, not activity: nights booked, messages sent to a reply, minutes of content actually watched, weekly active teams performing the core action.

Properties of a good north star: it moves when users get value, it is hard to game without genuinely helping users, it is measurable frequently, and teams can influence it. Most orgs then decompose it into input metrics teams can actually move.

## Connecting design to business outcomes

The chain you should be able to state for any project:

```
design change → behaviour change → funnel-step change → business outcome
```

Example: "Moving verification after first value → fewer users blocked at signup → +8 points activation → +X retained users at day 30 → estimated ₹Y annual revenue at current ARPU."

Every link is arguable; state your assumptions and the confidence level. A defensible chain with stated assumptions beats a confident number nobody believes.

## Cost-side metrics designers can move

Often easier to prove than revenue, and taken just as seriously:
- Support contacts per 1,000 users (or per order)
- Time-to-resolution for common issues
- Error rate and failed-transaction rate
- Manual operations time (internal tools are usually full of this)
- Training time for new staff on internal products
- Refunds, returns, and chargebacks

For internal tools, **time saved × loaded hourly cost × number of staff** is a straightforward and persuasive calculation.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "How does your design work connect to business value?"
- "What is a north-star metric?"
- "Our activation is 20%. What would you do?"

**For the activation question**, show a structured approach: "First I would define what activation means here and check it is the right definition — often the event chosen is a proxy for value rather than value itself. Then I would break the path into steps and find where the 80% are lost, segmented by platform, channel, and geography, because a single average usually hides one broken segment. Then I would separate the three causes: people who cannot (usability and technical failures), people who will not (the value is not clear or the ask is too early), and people who should not (we acquired the wrong users, which is an acquisition problem no onboarding change will fix). Each has a different fix, and the third is the one teams most often miss while redesigning screens."

That three-way split — cannot / will not / should not — is a memorable, reusable framework.

## Check yourself

1. Name the AARRR stages and the one where design has the most leverage.
2. What does a flattening retention curve indicate?
3. Give the properties of a good north-star metric.
4. Write the four-link chain from a design change to a business outcome.
5. Give the three-way split for diagnosing low activation.
