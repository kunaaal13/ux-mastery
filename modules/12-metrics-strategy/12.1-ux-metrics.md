# 12.1 UX Metrics

## In one line

Pick metrics that would change your decision if they moved — everything else is reporting.

## The HEART framework (Google)

| Dimension | What it measures | Example metrics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Happiness** | Attitudinal satisfaction | SUS, CSAT, NPS, app-store rating |
| **Engagement** | Depth of involvement per user | Sessions per week, actions per session, time in core task |
| **Adoption** | New users taking up the product/feature | New users completing first key action, feature uptake rate |
| **Retention** | Users returning over time | D7/D30 retention, churn rate, resurrection rate |
| **Task success** | Efficiency and effectiveness | Completion rate, time on task, error rate |

**Do not measure all five.** Choose the one or two that matter for the specific product stage and question. Engagement is the most misused: for a tax-filing tool, more time in the product is a failure signal, not a success one.

## Goals → Signals → Metrics (GSM)

The companion process, and the part people skip:

1. **Goal** — what are we trying to achieve, in user terms? ("Users file confidently without help.")
2. **Signal** — what observable behaviour would indicate progress? ("Fewer support contacts; fewer abandoned returns; fewer edits after submission.")
3. **Metric** — how do we count it? ("Support contacts per 100 filings; abandonment rate at step 4; edit rate within 24 hours.")

Going straight to metrics produces numbers nobody can interpret. Doing GSM out loud in an interview is a strong, concrete answer to "how would you measure success?"

## Task-level metrics

- **Task success rate** — the fundamental usability metric. Define "success" precisely and in advance, including partial success.
- **Time on task** — directional only; interpret with success. Faster failure is not an improvement.
- **Error rate** — errors per task, and severity of each.
- **SEQ** — one post-task question, cheap and sensitive.
- **Completion by segment** — averages conceal the population you are failing.

## Attitudinal instruments

- **SUS** — 0–100, ~68 average, comparable across products and time; the best single benchmark instrument.
- **CSAT** — transactional satisfaction; good for specific interactions.
- **CES** — perceived effort; correlates with loyalty in service contexts.
- **NPS** — widely used in business, statistically crude, and useless as a diagnostic. Track it if the org does; never design from it alone.

## Choosing metrics well

Three questions before adopting any metric:

1. **Would a change in this number change what we do?** If not, it is reporting, not measurement.
2. **What would make this number improve for a bad reason?** Time on task rising can mean engagement or confusion; sessions rising can mean people cannot finish in one go. Name the perverse path before you commit.
3. **Who is excluded from this number?** Analytics only sees people who arrived and whose events fired. The users who could not sign up at all are invisible in every product metric.

**Pair counter-metrics.** Any primary metric should be balanced by a guardrail that catches the cheap way to move it: conversion paired with refund rate; engagement paired with satisfaction; speed paired with error rate.

## Vanity vs. actionable

Vanity: total registered users, page views, raw downloads, cumulative anything. They only go up and cannot inform a decision.
Actionable: rates, cohort-based figures, per-user values, and funnel step conversion — numbers that can go down and tell you why.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "How would you measure the success of this design?"
- "What is HEART?"
- "This metric went up but users complain. What is happening?"

**Answer structure for "how would you measure success":** run GSM out loud. "The goal is X in user terms. The signals would be A and B. So I would measure A as [rate], with a guardrail on C so we would notice if we moved A by making something else worse. I would also state what the metric cannot see — anyone who never reached this screen is not in it."

That is a complete, senior answer in four sentences, and it works for any prompt.
