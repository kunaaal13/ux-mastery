# 1.2 The Design Process

## In one line

Good process alternates between **widening** (explore possibilities) and **narrowing** (commit to one), and the most common failure is skipping straight to narrow.

## Basic

### The Double Diamond (British Design Council, 2005)

Four phases, two diamonds:

```
   DISCOVER        DEFINE          DEVELOP         DELIVER
  (diverge)      (converge)       (diverge)      (converge)
      \            /                  \             /
       \          /                    \           /
        \        /                      \         /
      problem space                  solution space
```

- **Discover** — research the problem. Interviews, observation, analytics, support tickets. Output: raw findings.
- **Define** — synthesise into *one* framed problem. Output: a problem statement or "How Might We" question.
- **Develop** — generate many solutions. Sketches, flows, prototypes, competitor teardowns. Output: candidate concepts.
- **Deliver** — test, refine, and ship one. Output: a validated design and a measurement plan.

The critical idea: **the first diamond is about the problem, the second about the solution.** Most teams begin at "Develop" because someone already decided the feature. If you can name which diamond a project skipped, you can explain most product failures.

### Design Thinking (IDEO / Stanford d.school)

Empathise → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test. Same skeleton, different vocabulary, more emphasis on empathy and rapid prototyping. Non-linear: testing routinely throws you back to Define.

### Lean UX

Build–Measure–Learn. Start from an **assumption**, express it as a testable hypothesis, build the smallest thing that tests it, measure, and either persevere or pivot. Emphasises shared understanding over documentation: a whiteboard everyone saw beats a spec nobody read.

Hypothesis template:

> We believe that **[building this]** for **[these people]** will achieve **[this outcome]**. We will know we are right when we see **[this signal]**.

### Agile reality

Real teams run dual-track: a discovery track (research, framing, validation) running ahead of a delivery track (build, ship). Design work is not a sprint-zero event; it runs continuously one to two sprints ahead.

## Deeper

### Why the diverge/converge rhythm matters

Two cognitive traps:

- **Premature convergence** — the first plausible idea anchors the team, and everything after is a variation of it. Countermeasure: force a quota of distinct concepts (six, not three) before evaluating any.
- **Endless divergence** — exploring forever because committing feels risky. Countermeasure: timebox and pre-commit to a decision criterion before the exploration starts.

### Where each process breaks

| Process | Breaks when | Symptom |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Double Diamond | Treated as strictly sequential | 6-week "research phase" with no shipped learning |
| Design Thinking | Empathy becomes performative | Personas nobody reads, workshops with no decision |
| Lean UX | Metric chosen badly | Local optimisation; engagement up, satisfaction down |
| Agile | Design squeezed into a sprint | Design becomes ticket-shaped decoration |

### Fidelity as a scheduling decision

Match fidelity to the question you are answering:

| Question | Right fidelity |
| --- | --- |
| Is this the right problem? | Words. A problem statement. No artwork. |
| Does the flow make sense? | Paper or grey-box wireframes |
| Is the hierarchy readable? | Mid-fi with real content, no colour |
| Does the interaction feel right? | Clickable prototype with motion |
| Will it convert? | Live A/B test |

Raising fidelity too early is expensive because it invites feedback about colour when you needed feedback about structure.

## Applications

**Example — reframing in Define.** A banking team was asked to "redesign the transfer screen" after complaints. Discovery showed the screen tested fine; users abandoned because they could not remember the payee's account number. The reframed problem — "how might we let people pay someone without knowing their account number" — produced a contacts-based payee flow. Same brief, different diamond.

**Do this / not that**

- *Not that:* Presenting one polished concept in the first review.
- *Do this:* Presenting three concepts at deliberately different points on a trade-off axis (fastest to build / safest / most ambitious) and naming which you recommend and why.

## Interview angle

**"Walk me through your design process."** This is the single most common UX interview question. A weak answer recites the double diamond. A strong answer does three things:

1. **States the skeleton in one sentence** — "I work in two loops: framing the problem, then exploring the solution."
2. **Immediately grounds it in a real project** — walk through *one* project along that skeleton, naming what you did at each step and, crucially, what you *learned that changed the plan*.
3. **Names how you adapted it** — "On a two-week deadline I compressed discovery into six user calls and one analytics pass, and I flagged the risk of skipping the diary study."

**The trap:** describing an idealised process you have never run. Interviewers probe with "what would you cut if you had one week?" If your process has no compression story, it reads as theoretical.

**Follow-ups to be ready for**
- "What if there is no research budget?" — proxy research: support tickets, sales-call recordings, app-store reviews, session recordings, internal staff who talk to users daily, and five guerrilla tests.
- "How do you know when to stop iterating?" — pre-committed success criteria plus diminishing returns in test findings (when two consecutive sessions surface no new issue).
- "What if the PM already decided the solution?" — agree to build it, but negotiate a cheap validation step and a metric; disagree-and-commit with evidence collection attached.
