# 1.4 Usability, Utility, Desirability

## In one line

Usability asks "can they use it?", utility asks "is it worth using?", desirability asks "do they want it?" — and a product needs all three.

## Basic

### The distinction that matters most

- **Utility** — does it provide the functionality people need? A product with perfect usability and no utility is a beautifully polished thing nobody needs.
- **Usability** — can people accomplish their goals effectively, efficiently, and with satisfaction? (That triad is the ISO 9241-11 definition.)
- **Useful = utility + usability.** Nielsen's formulation.
- **Desirability** — the emotional pull: brand, aesthetics, identity, status.

### Nielsen's five usability components

1. **Learnability** — how easy is the first attempt?
2. **Efficiency** — once learned, how fast can they go?
3. **Memorability** — after time away, can they return without relearning?
4. **Errors** — how many, how severe, how recoverable?
5. **Satisfaction** — how pleasant is it?

These trade off against each other. A command line scores badly on learnability and superbly on efficiency; a wizard inverts that. Choosing which to optimise is a product decision about frequency of use: **optimise learnability for occasional tasks, efficiency for daily ones.**

### Peter Morville's UX Honeycomb

Useful, Usable, Findable, Credible, Desirable, Accessible, and — at the centre — Valuable. Its practical use is as a checklist for *why* an otherwise fine design underperforms: often the failure is Findable (nobody discovers the feature) or Credible (the design does not look trustworthy enough to enter a card number).

## Deeper

### Why usable products still fail

- **No utility.** Solved a problem nobody has.
- **Not findable.** The feature exists three levels deep; discovery is the bottleneck, not usability.
- **Not credible.** Trust signals missing: no reviews, unclear pricing, aggressive permission requests before value.
- **Not adoptable.** Switching cost exceeds the improvement. A 20% better product rarely beats an entrenched one — the incumbent has the user's data, habits, and social graph. Rule of thumb: you need to be several times better on a dimension the user actually cares about, not marginally better overall.
- **Wrong moment.** Correct solution, wrong point in the journey.

### Satisfaction is not a soft metric

Emotional response predicts return usage and word of mouth. The Peak–End Rule (see `03-ux-laws`) means the *remembered* experience is dominated by the most intense moment and the ending — which is why a rough middle with a delightful confirmation screen can out-rate a uniformly average flow.

### Measuring each

| Property | Measure with |
| --- | --- |
| Utility | Adoption rate, retention, "how disappointed would you be if this disappeared" (Sean Ellis test) |
| Usability | Task success rate, time on task, error rate, SUS score, SEQ |
| Findability | Findability rate in tree tests, search-vs-navigate ratio |
| Credibility | Drop-off at trust moments (payment, permissions), qualitative probes |
| Desirability | Product reaction cards, NPS as a directional signal, brand lift |

**SUS (System Usability Scale)** — 10 items, score 0–100; ~68 is average, 80+ is good. Handy because it is comparable across products and time.

## Applications

**Example — utility over usability.** Early Craigslist scores terribly on visual craft and modern usability conventions, yet dominated classifieds for years because its utility (liquidity: buyers and sellers actually there) outweighed interface friction. Lesson: do not diagnose a marketplace problem as a UI problem.

**Example — credibility.** A checkout that asks for card details before showing total shipping cost has no usability defect — every field is labelled and validated — and still leaks conversion, because the user does not trust what happens next. The fix is sequencing and disclosure, not the form.

**Do this / not that**

- *Not that:* Running a usability test on a feature to find out whether people want it.
- *Do this:* Testing desirability and utility first (fake-door, concept test, interviews), usability second.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "How do you measure whether a design is successful?"
- "A feature tests well in usability studies but nobody uses it. What do you do?"

**Answer for the second** — this is a diagnosis question, and the strong answer separates the funnel:

1. **Awareness** — do people know it exists? Check entry-point impressions.
2. **Comprehension** — do they understand what it does? Check the label and value proposition.
3. **Motivation** — do they want the outcome? Check with interviews, not tests.
4. **Ability** — can they complete it? *This* is what usability testing covered, and it passed.
5. **Trigger** — is there a moment that prompts use?

"Usability testing validated step 4 only. The failure is almost certainly at 1, 2, or 3, and each has a different fix — placement, copy, or the feature should not exist." That structure demonstrates you know what a method can and cannot prove.
