# 1.1 What Is UX?

## In one line

User experience is the *whole* of a person's perceptions and responses resulting from the use or anticipated use of a product, system, or service — not just the screen.

## Basic

The formal definition comes from ISO 9241-210: UX includes users' emotions, beliefs, preferences, perceptions, physical and psychological responses, behaviours, and accomplishments that occur **before, during, and after** use.

Read that list again and notice what is *not* in it: buttons, colours, screens. Those are means. The experience is the outcome.

**Everyday analogy.** A restaurant. The UI is the menu design and the plating. The UX is: how hard it was to book, whether the door was heavy, how the host greeted you, how long the food took, whether the bill was confusing, and whether you would come back. A beautiful menu cannot rescue a 50-minute wait.

Don Norman, who coined the term at Apple in the early 1990s, chose it deliberately because "human interface" and "usability" were too narrow — he wanted a word that covered industrial design, graphics, the interface, physical interaction, and the manual.

## Deeper

### UX is a system property, not a layer

You cannot "add UX" at the end, because the experience is produced by the whole system: pricing, latency, support policy, onboarding email, error copy, and refund flow all move it. This is why UX work that only touches the visual layer plateaus quickly — the biggest wins usually sit in policy and architecture, not pixels.

### The three overlapping fields

- **UX design** — the structure of the experience: flows, hierarchy, behaviour, research.
- **UI design** — the visual and interactive surface: layout, type, colour, components, states.
- **Product design** — UX + UI + business framing: what to build, for whom, and why it is worth building.

Titles vary wildly by company. Treat the title as a hint and read the job description for the actual scope: does it mention research and metrics (product/UX-leaning) or components and design systems (UI/craft-leaning)?

### Related roles you will collide with

| Role | Owns |
| --- | --- |
| UX researcher | Study design, recruiting, synthesis, insight repository |
| Content designer / UX writer | Voice, microcopy, information sequencing, error language |
| Design technologist / design engineer | Prototypes in real code, design-system implementation |
| Service designer | Journeys crossing channels — app, call centre, physical, email |
| Product manager | Prioritisation, scope, business case, stakeholder alignment |

### What UX is *not*

- **Not "making it pretty."** Aesthetics matter (see the Aesthetic–Usability Effect in `03-ux-laws`) but they are one input.
- **Not "what users say they want."** Users report preferences unreliably and predict their own future behaviour badly. Research is about observed behaviour and underlying needs, not order-taking.
- **Not the designer's taste.** Taste is a fast prior. Evidence overrides it.

## Applications

**Product example — Amazon 1-Click.** The interface change was trivial: one button. The experience change was structural — it removed the entire checkout funnel for repeat buyers by moving payment and address decisions earlier in time. The UX win lived in the data model and the policy (stored payment, default address), not the button.

**Product example — Uber's map.** The core anxiety of a taxi is "is it coming?" Showing a moving car on a map did not make hailing faster; it made waiting tolerable. That is a UX intervention on emotion, not on efficiency.

**Do this / not that**

- *Not that:* "The signup page converts badly, let me redesign the form."
- *Do this:* "The signup page converts badly. Let me first check whether people arriving even want an account yet, and whether we can defer signup until after first value."

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "What is UX design?" (Screening question. They are testing vocabulary and breadth.)
- "What is the difference between UX and UI?"
- "Is UX just common sense?"

**How to answer "What is UX design?"** — 60 to 90 seconds, three beats:

1. **Definition with scope.** "UX is the whole of what a person perceives and feels before, during, and after using a product — so it covers the flow, the content, the timing, and the recovery paths, not only the screen."
2. **A contrast that shows you know the boundary.** "UI is the surface I hand to engineering. UX is the decision about what surface should exist at all."
3. **A concrete example from your own work.** "On [project], the reported problem was a confusing screen. The actual problem was that we asked for a phone number before the user had any reason to trust us. Moving that step after first value raised completion by X%."

That third beat is what separates candidates. Definitions are cheap; a lived example is proof.

**Answering "Is UX just common sense?"** — do not get defensive. "Often it *looks* obvious in hindsight, which is a sign the design worked. But common sense is not shared: I have watched five users interpret the same icon five different ways. Research replaces my assumption with their behaviour."
