# 1.3 Roles and Artifacts

## In one line

Every deliverable exists to answer a question or align a group of people — if it does neither, it is decoration.

## Basic

### The artifacts, and the question each one answers

| Artifact | Question it answers | Fails when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Persona | Who are we designing for, and what do they need? | Built from demographics instead of behaviour |
| Jobs-to-be-done statement | What progress is the user trying to make? | Written as a feature request |
| Empathy map | What does this person say, think, do, feel? | Made up rather than sourced from research |
| User journey map | Where does the experience break across time? | Drawn only for the happy path |
| Service blueprint | What backstage process supports each front-stage moment? | Skips systems and staff actions |
| Task flow / user flow | What are the steps and branches? | Missing error and empty states |
| Sitemap | How is content organised? | Mirrors the org chart, not user mental models |
| Wireframe | What is on the page and in what priority? | Contains colour arguments |
| Prototype | Does the interaction work? | Higher fidelity than the question requires |
| Spec / redlines | What exactly does engineering build? | Written when a design system already answers it |
| Research report | What did we learn and what should change? | 40 slides, no recommendation |

### Persona vs. Job-to-be-done

A persona describes a *person*: "Priya, 32, product manager, commutes 45 minutes, values control." A JTBD describes a *situation*: "When I am commuting and my hands are busy, help me capture a thought so I do not lose it."

JTBD travels better across teams because it is falsifiable and situation-anchored. Personas are useful when the audiences genuinely differ in goals — a marketplace with buyers and sellers, for example. Personas built purely from demographics tend to be fiction with a stock photo.

### User journey map anatomy

Columns are stages over time. Rows typically include: user actions, touchpoints, thoughts, emotions (a line graph), pain points, and opportunities. The value is the *dips*: a journey map with a flat emotion line was not researched.

## Deeper

### Choosing an artifact

Ask: *what decision is blocked right now?*

- Blocked on **who** → JTBD or persona
- Blocked on **where it hurts** → journey map, backed by research
- Blocked on **what happens when** → flow diagram including errors
- Blocked on **what it looks like** → wireframe or prototype
- Blocked on **whether it works** → usability test, not another artifact

Producing artifacts nobody requested is the most common way junior designers burn credibility. Producing exactly the artifact that unblocks a stuck decision is how you earn scope.

### Working with adjacent roles

- **With PM:** you own how, they own why-now and priority; you both own what. Bring options with trade-offs, not a single answer, when strategy is genuinely open.
- **With engineering:** involve them *during* exploration, not after. Ask "what is cheap and what is expensive here?" before you fall in love with a concept. A design that costs three sprints when a variant costs three days needs a reason.
- **With research:** if you have a dedicated researcher, your job is to bring sharp questions and to actually change the design when findings arrive. If you do not, you are the researcher.
- **With content design:** bring them in at wireframe stage. Copy is not filler applied at the end; the words usually *are* the interface.
- **With data/analytics:** define the measurement plan before launch, not after. "We will look at the data later" means nobody instrumented the event.

### RACI in practice

For a typical feature: Design is **Responsible** for the solution, PM is **Accountable** for the outcome, Engineering and Research are **Consulted** early, Support and Marketing are **Informed** before launch — and should be Consulted if the change alters what users must be told.

## Applications

**Example — the journey map that changed a roadmap.** A telecom mapped the "move house" journey and found the emotional low was not in the app at all: it was a 9-day gap between disconnection and reconnection, invisible in every screen. The design fix was a status page plus proactive SMS — no new app feature. That is what a well-made journey map buys you.

**Do this / not that**

- *Not that:* Making three personas because the process template has a persona box.
- *Do this:* Making one JTBD statement per audience segment, each traceable to at least three interview quotes.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "What deliverables do you produce?"
- "How do you work with product managers and engineers?"
- "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a PM."

**How to answer "what deliverables do you produce?"** — resist listing. Say: "It depends on what decision is stuck," then give two examples where you deliberately produced *less* than expected because the decision was cheap, and one where you produced a heavier artifact because alignment across teams was the actual problem. That framing signals judgement rather than output.

**Disagreement questions** want to see: you argued with evidence, you sought the underlying goal rather than defending your pixels, you committed once a decision was made, and you attached a way to learn who was right.

## Check yourself

1. Give the difference between a persona and a JTBD statement in one sentence each.
2. What part of a journey map carries the most decision value, and why?
3. When is a spec redundant?
4. Name the artifact you would build to unblock "we cannot agree who this is for."
