# 2.3 Attention & Perception

## In one line

Users do not read screens, they scan them — and what gets noticed is decided pre-consciously by a handful of visual attributes.

## Basic

### Pre-attentive attributes

Some visual properties are processed in under ~250 ms, before conscious attention, in parallel across the whole visual field. These are the only tools that make something "pop" without the user searching:

| Category | Attributes |
| --- | --- |
| **Form** | Size, shape, orientation, length, width, curvature, added marks, enclosure |
| **Colour** | Hue, intensity/saturation |
| **Position** | 2D position, spatial grouping |
| **Motion** | Flicker, direction of movement |

Two consequences:

1. **Encode importance with a pre-attentive attribute.** Size and colour intensity are the workhorses.
2. **Use one at a time.** If everything is emphasised, nothing is. A page with four "primary" buttons has zero primary buttons — the pop-out effect collapses when the distinguishing attribute is shared by many items.

### Scanning patterns

- **F-pattern** — dense text-heavy pages with poor formatting. Two horizontal sweeps at the top, then a vertical scan down the left edge. It is a symptom of unformatted content, not a layout to design toward.
- **Z-pattern / layer-cake** — sparse pages with clear headings; the eye jumps heading to heading.
- **Spotted pattern** — users hunting for a specific known item (a price, a date, a link) skip everything else.

Design implication: **front-load meaning.** Put the informative words first in headings, links, and list items, because the first two words carry the scan.

### Change blindness and banner blindness

- **Change blindness** — people fail to notice large changes to a scene, especially across an interruption or if the change is not in the attentional focus. This is why a success message that appears far from the user's click often goes unseen.
- **Banner blindness** — users learn to ignore regions and shapes that look like advertising: right rails, wide colourful rectangles, carousels. Making an important message look like an ad guarantees it is unread.
- **Inattentional blindness** — focused users miss the obvious; a user hunting for "Checkout" will scan past a large promotional banner entirely.

## Deeper

### Selective attention has a cost structure

Attention is a bottleneck. Every additional competing element does not just add noise; it lengthens visual search. Search time grows roughly linearly with the number of distractors that share the target's features, and stays flat when the target differs on a pre-attentive dimension. Practical translation: **make the thing you want found differ on one strong dimension, and reduce the count of look-alike items.**

### The cost of motion

Movement is the strongest pre-attentive attribute — it captures attention involuntarily. That makes it powerful and dangerous:

- Use for genuinely urgent, rare events (a live error, an incoming call).
- Never for ambient decoration next to content that requires reading.
- Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`; for some users motion causes nausea or is disabling (see Module 10).

### Signal detection: the false-alarm problem

Every alert design sets a threshold. Too sensitive and users get false alarms, learn the alert is noise, and ignore it — alarm fatigue. Too conservative and real events are missed. Notification design is a threshold decision, not a visual one: ask "what fraction of these will matter to the user?" before asking what the badge looks like.

### The fold, revisited

"Nobody scrolls" is obsolete, but attention is still heavily weighted above the fold — the material at the top receives disproportionate viewing time, and content below is seen by a declining share of visitors. The honest formulation: **scrolling is free, but attention is not.** Put the strongest reason to continue at the top, and make it visually obvious the page continues.

## Applications

**Example — one primary button.** A dashboard with "Export," "Share," "Publish," and "Settings" all rendered as filled buttons has destroyed its own hierarchy. Making only "Publish" filled, the rest ghost or text, is a pre-attentive fix that costs nothing.

**Example — a change nobody sees.** Deleting an item at the bottom of a long list shows a toast at the top of the viewport. Half of users never register it and delete again. Fix: place the feedback near the locus of attention (inline, at the row), or use an undo affordance that appears where the action occurred.

**Do this / not that**

- *Not that:* Communicating status with colour alone (red/green).
- *Do this:* Colour **plus** shape or text — redundant encoding, which also handles colour-vision deficiency (Module 10).

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "How do you draw attention to the most important element?"
- "Users are missing our new feature announcement. Why?"
- "Critique this screen's hierarchy."

**Answering the announcement question** — a strong answer runs the perception checklist: Is it in a region users have learned to ignore (banner blindness)? Does it appear during a focused task (inattentional blindness)? Does it change without a transition the user can perceive (change blindness)? Does it differ from surroundings on a pre-attentive attribute, or does it just say "important"? Then: "I would rather place it in the path of the task than shout louder next to it."

**For hierarchy critique**, narrate in order: "What does my eye hit first, second, third? Does that order match the user's priority? What attribute creates each step — size, weight, colour, spacing, position?" That vocabulary is exactly what a design-craft interviewer is listening for.
