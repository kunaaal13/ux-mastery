# 13.2 Ethics & Dark Patterns

## In one line

A dark pattern is an interface deliberately designed to make users do something they did not intend — and it is now a regulatory category, not just an ethical complaint.

## The taxonomy

Building on Harry Brignull's original work and later regulatory classifications:

| Pattern | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Roach motel** | Easy to get in, hard to get out (subscribe in one click, cancel by phone) |
| **Sneak into basket** | Items or add-ons added without clear consent |
| **Hidden costs / drip pricing** | Fees revealed only at the final step |
| **Confirmshaming** | Guilt-laden decline options ("No thanks, I hate saving money") |
| **Misdirection** | Visual hierarchy steering to the business-preferred option |
| **Trick questions** | Double negatives, inverted checkbox logic |
| **Forced continuity** | Silent charging after a trial with no reminder |
| **Privacy Zuckering** | Manipulating users into sharing more than intended |
| **Bait and switch** | An action produces a different result than advertised |
| **Disguised ads** | Adverts styled as content or controls |
| **Nagging** | Repeated interruption until the user relents |
| **Obstruction** | Deliberate friction on the path the user wants |
| **Preselection** | Business-favourable options checked by default |
| **False urgency / scarcity** | Fabricated countdowns and stock counts |

## Regulation

This is no longer only a matter of conscience:

- **EU:** the GDPR requires freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous consent — pre-ticked boxes and cookie walls where "reject" is harder than "accept" have been repeatedly ruled non-compliant. The Digital Services Act explicitly prohibits dark patterns on covered platforms; the Digital Markets Act adds obligations for gatekeepers.
- **US:** the FTC has acted on negative-option billing and deceptive design; several states have consumer-privacy statutes addressing manipulative consent.
- **India:** the CCPA (Central Consumer Protection Authority) issued guidelines specifically listing and prohibiting dark patterns in 2023, and the DPDP Act governs consent for personal data.
- **Everywhere:** "click to cancel"-style requirements — cancellation must be as easy as subscription — are spreading.

Knowing that dark patterns carry legal exposure changes the conversation from a values debate to a risk assessment, which is often how you actually win it in a company.

## A usable ethical framework

**The three questions** (from 13.1): whose goal does it serve, would I be comfortable explaining it, is the reverse as easy as the forward?

**Additional lenses:**
- **The regret test** — will the user, on discovering how this worked, feel deceived?
- **The vulnerable-user test** — how does this behave for someone distracted, in debt, in crisis, cognitively impaired, or unfamiliar with the language? Manipulative design harms unevenly.
- **The scale test** — a nudge that is mildly unfair at n=1 becomes a systemic harm at n=10 million.
- **The publication test** — would this design survive being described accurately in a news article?

## Beyond dark patterns: the wider ethics surface

- **Attention** — is the product's success metric aligned with users' wellbeing, or does it profit from compulsive use?
- **Data** — collecting the minimum, being clear about use, making deletion real and easy.
- **Algorithmic effects** — ranking and recommendation shape behaviour and belief; "we just show what people engage with" is a design decision, not a neutral fact.
- **Accessibility as ethics** — excluding people is a harm regardless of intent (Module 10).
- **Environmental cost** — data transfer, model inference, and device churn have real footprints.
- **Inclusion in defaults** — name fields, gender options, address formats, and honorifics encode assumptions about who counts as a normal user.

## What to do when asked to build one

1. **Establish the facts** — is the mechanism accurate (is the stock really low)? An inaccurate claim is usually a legal problem, and that framing ends most conversations quickly.
2. **Name the risk in the org's currency** — regulatory exposure, refund and chargeback rates, support cost, app-store or platform policy violation, and brand damage.
3. **Offer the honest alternative that serves the same business goal** — real scarcity communicated accurately, a genuine deadline, a better default with an easy change, clearer value framing. Most business goals behind a dark pattern have an honest route.
4. **Propose measurement that would catch the harm** — refunds, cancellations, complaints, and support contacts, not just the conversion lift.
5. **If overruled on something you believe is deceptive,** say so clearly once, in writing, and escalate through the appropriate channel. Document. There is a line where "disagree and commit" does not apply, and knowingly deceiving users is close to it.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "What would you do if asked to design something you think is unethical?"
- "Is a countdown timer a dark pattern?"
- "Where is the line between persuasion and manipulation?"

**Answer the first without grandstanding.** Interviewers are testing whether you can handle the situation professionally, not whether you have strong opinions: "I would start by understanding the goal behind the request, because there is usually an honest way to reach it. Then I would name the risks concretely — regulatory exposure, refunds and chargebacks, support cost, and app-store policy — and propose an alternative that serves the same goal. If the answer is still to proceed and the design genuinely deceives users, I would say so once clearly, in writing, and escalate. I would not treat it as a reason to be difficult about the rest of the work, but that particular line I would not quietly cross."
