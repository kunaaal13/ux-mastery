# 8.3 Validating IA

## In one line

IA is the easiest part of UX to validate cheaply, and the part most often shipped on opinion.

## Card sorting — "how do users group things?"

Participants sort content items into groups.

- **Open sort** — participants create and name their own categories. Use when designing a new structure; the *names they invent* are as valuable as the groupings.
- **Closed sort** — categories are fixed; participants place items into them. Use to validate or refine an existing structure.
- **Hybrid** — fixed categories plus the ability to add new ones.

Practical notes: 15–30 participants gives stable results; use 30–60 items (more causes fatigue); run it unmoderated for scale, moderated for a handful of sessions to hear the reasoning — the reasoning is often the real finding. Analyse with a similarity matrix and dendrogram, but treat clusters as evidence, not instructions.

**What it tells you:** users' mental models and vocabulary.
**What it does not tell you:** whether people can *find* things in your structure. That is a different question.

## Tree testing — "can users find things in this structure?"

Participants are given a task ("Where would you go to change your delivery address?") and navigate a text-only version of the hierarchy — no visual design, no search. It isolates the structure.

Metrics:
- **Success rate** — did they end at the right node?
- **Directness** — did they get there without backtracking? Low directness with high success means the label is ambiguous even though the structure is workable.
- **Time** and **first click**.

Run it before you design screens; fixing IA in a tree test costs hours, fixing it after launch costs quarters.

## First-click testing

Show a screen (or wireframe) and ask where they would click first for a given task. The evidence is strong that a correct first click is a powerful predictor of eventual task success — users who start down the wrong branch often never recover. Cheap, fast, and works on static images.

## Findability and other measures

- **Findability rate** — percentage who locate a given item without search.
- **Search-vs-navigate ratio** — a rising share of sessions using search often means navigation is failing (though for large catalogues, search dominance is normal).
- **Pogo-sticking rate** — repeated in-and-out behaviour between a list and its items signals weak scent.
- **Zero-result queries** — a direct list of vocabulary gaps and missing content.
- **Support-ticket topics** — free, continuous evidence of what people cannot find.

## Reverse card sorting and content audits

- **Content audit / inventory** — enumerate everything: page, owner, traffic, last update, purpose. Boring, and the highest-leverage first step in any restructure. Typically reveals a large share of content with near-zero traffic, which changes the problem from "organise it all" to "retire most of it."
- **Content model** — define the *types* of content and their attributes and relationships. This is what makes an IA scale, and it is the part engineers most need from you.

## A validation sequence that works

1. Content inventory and analytics baseline
2. Users' vocabulary from search logs, tickets, interviews
3. Open card sort (15–30 users)
4. Draft 2–3 candidate structures
5. Tree test each (30–50 users per structure)
6. Refine labels; re-test the weakest tasks
7. First-click test on the designed screens
8. Ship with redirects; monitor findability, search ratio, and zero-result queries

Steps 3 and 5 answer different questions and neither substitutes for the other.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "How do you know your IA is right?"
- "What is the difference between card sorting and tree testing?"
- "How would you validate a navigation redesign with no budget?"

**The card sort vs. tree test distinction is a classic screening question.** One sentence each: "A card sort asks how users would group and name things — it is generative and tells me their model. A tree test asks whether users can find things in a structure I propose — it is evaluative and tells me whether my model works. I use the sort to design and the tree test to verify."

**No budget:** search logs, support tickets, and analytics cost nothing; a tree test can be run with a free tool on a spreadsheet-derived tree and 20 colleagues-of-colleagues; a first-click test can be run on a screenshot with five people in a hallway. Say this plainly — resourcefulness is the trait being tested.
