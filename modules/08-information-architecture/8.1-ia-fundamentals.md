# 8.1 IA Fundamentals

## In one line

Information architecture is the structural design of shared information environments — deciding what things are called, how they group, and how someone gets from a need to the right place.

## The four components (Rosenfeld & Morville)

1. **Organisation systems** — how content is grouped.
2. **Labelling systems** — what things are called.
3. **Navigation systems** — how users move through.
4. **Search systems** — how users query.

Most IA failures are labelling failures wearing a navigation costume.

## Organisation schemes

**Exact (objective, unambiguous):**
- Alphabetical — good when users know the name (a directory, a country list).
- Chronological — good for events, news, versions.
- Geographical — good when location is the primary filter.

**Ambiguous (subjective, requires judgement — and far more useful):**
- **Topical** — by subject. The most common, and the hardest to get right.
- **Task-oriented** — by what the user wants to do ("Send money", "Track a claim"). Usually beats topical for transactional products.
- **Audience-based** — "For students / For teachers". Works only when users can confidently self-identify and belong to exactly one group.
- **Metaphor-driven** — desktop, cart, folders. Powerful but constraining; the metaphor eventually breaks.

Real products use **hybrids**, and the risk of hybrids is inconsistency — a nav with three topical items, one task item, and one audience item makes users guess which logic applies.

## Structures

| Structure | Shape | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hierarchy (tree)** | Parent/child | Most products; matches users' expectations |
| **Sequence** | Linear steps | Checkouts, onboarding, courses |
| **Matrix** | Multiple simultaneous paths (by price, by brand, by size) | Catalogues with faceted filtering |
| **Network / hypertext** | Arbitrary links | Wikis, knowledge bases; poor as the only structure |
| **Database / faceted** | Metadata-driven retrieval | Large content sets where no single hierarchy fits |

**Breadth vs. depth.** Broad-and-shallow generally beats narrow-and-deep for findability: every extra level is a decision point at which users can go wrong, and recovering from a wrong branch is expensive. Practical guidance is to keep most content within about three levels, and note that this is about *decision points*, not about Miller's Law (Module 03).

## Labelling

- **Use the users' words**, gathered from research, search logs, and support tickets — not internal names. "Entity management" means nothing outside the building.
- **Be specific over clever.** "Solutions", "Resources", and "More" are the three least informative labels in common use.
- **Be parallel.** Items at the same level should share grammatical form: all nouns or all verbs, not a mix.
- **Front-load the meaningful word** — users scan the first two words of a link (Module 02).
- **Consistency across surfaces.** The label in navigation, in the page title, in search results, and in the email must match, or users cannot tell they arrived in the right place.

## Information scent

From information-foraging theory: users follow whichever link *smells* most like the information they want, judging by label, context, and surroundings. Practical consequences:

- **Strong scent beats short paths.** Users tolerate more clicks if every click confirms they are getting warmer. The "three-click rule" is not supported by evidence; scent is what predicts success.
- **Weak or misleading scent causes pogo-sticking** — the pattern of clicking into a page, bouncing back, and clicking a sibling. Pogo-sticking in analytics or recordings is a direct signal of a labelling or preview problem.
- **Show a preview of what is behind the link** — item counts, thumbnails, and short descriptions all strengthen scent.

## Common IA problems

| Problem | Symptom | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Org-chart IA | Sections mirror internal teams | Reorganise around user tasks, validated by card sort |
| Ambiguous overlaps | The same item plausibly fits two categories | Allow polyhierarchy (an item in two places) or clarify labels |
| Everything in "Other/More" | The catch-all keeps growing | Re-derive categories from actual content, not from what is left over |
| Nav grows by accretion | 14 items, none removed for years | Audit usage; demote low-traffic items to a secondary surface |
| Search that only matches titles | Users cannot find known content | Index body content and synonyms; log zero-result queries |

## Applications

**Example — task-oriented beats topical.** A bank's nav organised as "Accounts / Cards / Loans / Insurance" mirrors product lines. Users arrive wanting to *do* things: pay someone, dispute a charge, change an address. A task-oriented layer over the product taxonomy typically reduces support contacts more than any visual redesign.

**Do this / not that**
- *Not that:* One canonical location per item, enforced strictly.
- *Do this:* Allow an item to appear in more than one category when users' models genuinely disagree — cross-listing is cheap and matches how people actually look.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "How would you reorganise this product's navigation?"
- "How do you decide what goes in the main nav?"
- "Deep or shallow hierarchy?"

**Model process for a restructure:** (1) inventory the content and instrument current usage; (2) gather users' language from search logs, support tickets, and interviews; (3) run an **open card sort** to learn users' groupings; (4) draft two or three candidate structures; (5) validate with a **tree test** measuring task success and directness; (6) iterate and re-test; (7) ship with redirects, and measure search-vs-navigate ratio and zero-result queries afterwards.

Naming card sorting *and* tree testing, and knowing which answers which question, is the differentiator here — most candidates name only the first.
