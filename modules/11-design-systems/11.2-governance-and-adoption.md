# 11.2 Governance & Adoption

## In one line

Design systems fail for social reasons far more often than technical ones — the hard problems are contribution, trust, and adoption.

## Governance models

| Model | How it works | Fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Centralised** | A dedicated team owns everything | Consistency-critical, larger orgs; risks becoming a bottleneck |
| **Federated** | Representatives from product teams contribute and decide together | Mid-size orgs; risks slow decisions |
| **Open / community** | Anyone contributes, small core reviews | Strong engineering culture; risks incoherence without firm review |
| **Hybrid** | Core team owns foundations, product teams contribute components | The most common working answer |

Whatever the model, three things must be explicit: **who decides**, **how something gets in**, and **how a breaking change is communicated**.

## The contribution loop

1. **Propose** — a use case, not a component. "We need a way to show inline status on a row," not "we need a Chip."
2. **Check for an existing solution** — most requests are solved by an existing component used correctly, and this step is where a good system earns its keep.
3. **Prototype in the product** — build it locally first, use it in production, and let it settle.
4. **Promote** — when it has proven itself in two or three places, generalise, document, and move it into the system.
5. **Deprecate** — with a migration path, a codemod where possible, and a timeline. Systems that never remove anything become archives.

## Versioning and breaking changes

- Semantic versioning for the code library, with a human changelog.
- Breaking changes need lead time, a migration guide, and ideally automated migration. A breaking change without a codemod costs every consuming team, and teams remember that cost when deciding whether to adopt the next version.
- Deprecate before removing; keep the old path working through at least one release cycle.

## Adoption

Adoption is the only metric that matters. Systems are not adopted because they are mandated; they are adopted because using them is easier than not.

**Measure:**
- Percentage of product surfaces using system components (detectable in code).
- Percentage of design files using library components (detached-instance rate in Figma).
- Time-to-first-screen for a new designer or engineer.
- Number of one-off components created outside the system.
- Support-request volume and response time.

**Increase:**
- Make the system the path of least resistance — good defaults, clear docs, working examples, copy-paste snippets.
- Solve the consumers' actual pain first; the first components should be the ones teams are re-building most often.
- Respond fast to requests; a two-week wait teaches teams to build their own.
- Show the savings — hours not spent, bugs not filed, accessibility issues prevented.
- Run office hours and pair with teams rather than policing them.

## Why systems die

1. **Built without consumers.** A team designs a perfect library nobody asked for and it does not fit real product needs.
2. **Too slow to change.** Product teams ship around it, and once they have, they never return.
3. **No owner.** Contributors move on; the library rots and quietly becomes untrustworthy.
4. **Rigid where products need flexibility.** No escape hatch, so teams fork.
5. **Documentation that describes rather than guides.** Listing props without saying when to use what leaves every decision to be re-made.
6. **Measured by component count** rather than adoption — activity mistaken for value.

## The escape hatch

Every system needs a sanctioned way to do something the system does not cover — a documented "here is how to build a one-off, and here is how to propose it back." Without it, teams fork silently and drift becomes invisible.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "How would you get teams to adopt a design system?"
- "How do you handle a team that wants something different?"
- "How do you measure a design system's success?"

**For the diverging team:** "First I would find out what they actually need, because a request for a new component is usually a use case the system does not cover yet — and that is a gap in my product, not a compliance problem. If the existing component can serve it, I would fix the documentation, because if one team could not find it, others could not either. If it is a genuine gap, I would help them build it in their product first, then promote it once it has proven itself. The failure mode I want to avoid is saying no, because a team that is told no once builds their own from then on, and I lose visibility entirely."

**For measurement:** adoption rate in code and design, one-off component count, time-to-first-screen, and accessibility defects prevented — plus a plain statement that component count is not a success metric.
