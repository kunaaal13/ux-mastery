# 10.1 Accessibility Foundations

## In one line

Disability is a mismatch between a person and their environment — so an inaccessible interface is a design defect, not a user limitation.

## Models of disability

- **Medical model** — disability is an attribute of the person, to be treated. Leads to "we cannot support that user."
- **Social model** — disability arises from barriers in the environment. Leads to "our design creates this barrier; remove it."
- **Interaction/mismatch model** (used by inclusive-design practice) — disability is a mismatch between the person's capabilities and the design. Most useful for designers, because it makes the design the variable.

## The range of needs

| Category | Examples | Interface implications |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Visual** | Blindness, low vision, colour-vision deficiency | Screen-reader support, contrast, zoom to 200%+, never colour-only encoding |
| **Auditory** | Deafness, hard of hearing | Captions, transcripts, visual alternatives to sound alerts |
| **Motor** | Tremor, paralysis, RSI, one-handed use | Keyboard operability, large targets, no timing dependence, no precise dragging as the only method |
| **Cognitive** | Dyslexia, ADHD, memory, learning differences | Plain language, consistent structure, forgiving errors, no unnecessary time limits, reduced distraction |
| **Speech** | Speech differences | Never require voice as the only input |
| **Vestibular** | Motion sensitivity | Respect reduced-motion, avoid parallax and large auto-motion |
| **Seizure** | Photosensitive epilepsy | No flashing above three flashes per second |

**Permanent, temporary, and situational.** Microsoft's inclusive design framing: a person may have one arm (permanent), a broken arm (temporary), or be holding a baby (situational). All three benefit from one-handed operability. This reframing is the fastest way to make accessibility land with a sceptical stakeholder — the affected population is much larger than the disability statistics alone suggest.

**Scale:** roughly 15–16% of the world's population lives with a significant disability. Among men, around 8% have some form of colour-vision deficiency. Age-related impairments — vision, hearing, dexterity, memory — affect a growing share of users in every market.

## POUR — the four WCAG principles

1. **Perceivable** — users can perceive the information: text alternatives, captions, contrast, content not conveyed by sensory characteristics alone.
2. **Operable** — users can operate the interface: keyboard access, enough time, no seizure triggers, navigable structure, adequate target sizes.
3. **Understandable** — content and operation are comprehensible: readable language, predictable behaviour, input assistance and error recovery.
4. **Robust** — content works with current and future assistive technologies: valid semantics, correct names/roles/values.

Memorise POUR. It gives you a structure for any accessibility answer.

## Assistive technologies to know

- **Screen readers** (VoiceOver, NVDA, JAWS, TalkBack) — read the accessibility tree aloud; users navigate by headings, landmarks, links, and form controls, not by looking at layout.
- **Screen magnifiers** — often view a small portion at high zoom; content far from the point of focus is effectively invisible, which makes focus management critical.
- **Voice control** (Dragon, Voice Control) — users speak visible labels, so a button whose visible label differs from its accessible name becomes unusable ("label in name").
- **Switch access** — sequential navigation with one or two inputs; every extra focus stop costs real time.
- **Keyboard only** — a large and often-forgotten population, including many motor-impairment users and most power users.
- **Braille displays**, **captions**, **OS-level settings** (text size, bold text, reduce motion, increase contrast) — respect the platform settings rather than overriding them.

## The business and legal case

- **Legal:** many jurisdictions require it — the ADA (US, with WCAG used as the practical standard), Section 508 for US federal procurement, EN 301 549 in the EU, the European Accessibility Act, the UK Equality Act, and the Indian RPwD Act, among others. Litigation and procurement exclusions are real and increasing.
- **Market:** the affected population plus their households is a large share of the market; procurement in the public sector routinely requires conformance statements.
- **Quality:** accessible products are more usable for everyone — captions in noisy places, keyboard navigation for power users, plain language for non-native speakers, high contrast in sunlight.

Argue the ethical case first and the legal case second; the legal framing alone produces minimum compliance, which is the worst outcome — technically conformant and still unusable.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "How do you approach accessibility?"
- "Who benefits from accessible design?"
- "How would you convince a team that does not prioritise it?"

**Model answer to the first:** "I treat it as part of the definition of done rather than a review at the end, and I use POUR as the structure — perceivable, operable, understandable, robust. In practice, at design time that means contrast and never encoding meaning by colour alone, a full keyboard path with visible focus and a logical order, target sizes and spacing, plain language with error recovery, and specifying the semantics in handoff — names, roles, states, heading structure, and what gets announced. Then I test with a keyboard and a screen reader myself, and involve users with disabilities where the budget allows, because automated tools catch only a minority of issues."

**For convincing a team:** lead with the permanent/temporary/situational framing and one observed failure — watching a screen-reader user fail at your checkout is more persuasive than any statistic — then add procurement and legal exposure as the closing argument, not the opening one.
