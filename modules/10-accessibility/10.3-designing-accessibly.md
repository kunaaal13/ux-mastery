# 10.3 Designing Accessibly

## Design-time decisions

**Colour and contrast**
- Check every text/background pair against 4.5:1 (3:1 for large text) and every meaningful boundary or icon against 3:1.
- Design a greyscale pass: if the screen still works, nothing depends on hue alone.
- Do not forget disabled states, placeholder text, and text over images or video.

**Typography**
- Body text 16 px or larger; support OS text-size settings and dynamic type rather than fixing sizes.
- Layout must survive 200% zoom and increased text spacing — avoid fixed-height containers around text.
- Left-align body text; avoid justified text (rivers hurt dyslexic readers particularly).

**Keyboard and focus** — the part designers most often omit from specs:
- Specify tab order, and ensure it matches the visual reading order.
- Design the **focus indicator** explicitly; it must be visible against every background it can appear on and meet contrast requirements.
- Specify focus management for dialogs: focus moves into the dialog on open, is trapped inside while open, and returns to the trigger on close.
- Provide "skip to content" for repeated navigation.
- Any hover-revealed control needs a keyboard-reachable equivalent.

**Targets and gestures**
- 24×24 CSS px minimum (WCAG 2.2 AA); 44×44 pt is the better practical target on touch.
- Every drag interaction needs a single-pointer alternative (tap-to-select then tap-to-place, or menu-based move).
- Never require multi-finger or path-based gestures as the only way to do something.

**Motion**
- Respect `prefers-reduced-motion` with a variant that keeps the information (cross-fade or instant change).
- No auto-playing motion longer than 5 seconds without a pause control.

**Language and structure**
- Plain language, short sentences, expand jargon on first use.
- Real heading structure — do not use large bold text where a heading is meant; screen-reader users navigate by headings.
- Descriptive link text.
- Instructions must not rely on sensory characteristics ("click the round button on the right").

**Forms** (Module 07.4 applies almost entirely)
- Visible, persistent labels; never placeholder-as-label.
- Errors identified in text, associated with the field, and announced.
- Do not make users re-enter data they already gave (WCAG 2.2 3.3.7).
- Allow paste everywhere, especially in OTP and password fields.

## Specifying accessibility in handoff

An accessible build depends on information only the designer has. Include, per component:

- **Semantics** — what element it is (button vs link matters: links navigate, buttons act).
- **Accessible name** — including the visible label text.
- **Role and state** — expanded/collapsed, selected, checked, busy, invalid.
- **Focus behaviour** — order, trap, return, and what receives focus after an action.
- **Announcements** — what a screen reader should say when something changes asynchronously (a live region for "3 results found" or "Message sent").
- **Alt text** for every image, or explicitly marked decorative.

Handing over a screen without this is handing over half a design.

## Testing

Ordered by cost:

1. **Keyboard-only pass.** Unplug the mouse and complete the primary flow. Fastest and most revealing test available. If you cannot finish, neither can a whole population.
2. **Automated tools** (axe, Lighthouse, WAVE). Cheap and fast, but they detect only a minority of issues — roughly a third at best — and cannot judge whether alt text is meaningful or focus order is sensible. Never claim accessibility on an automated pass alone.
3. **Screen reader pass.** VoiceOver on macOS/iOS, NVDA on Windows, TalkBack on Android. Learn the basic navigation commands; an hour of practice pays for itself repeatedly.
4. **Zoom and reflow** — 200% zoom, 320 px width, increased text spacing.
5. **Contrast audit** across states, including disabled and hover.
6. **Usability testing with disabled users.** The only method that finds what actually matters. Recruit deliberately; standard panels under-represent assistive-technology users badly.

## Common failures ranked by damage

1. Keyboard traps and missing focus indicators — blocks entire populations.
2. Custom controls with no name/role/state — a screen reader announces "clickable" and nothing else.
3. Colour-only status encoding.
4. Placeholder-as-label.
5. Insufficient contrast, especially grey-on-white body text and disabled states.
6. Auto-playing motion with no control.
7. Time limits that cannot be extended.
8. Images with missing or useless alt text ("image1.png").
9. Modals that do not trap or restore focus.
10. Content changes announced to nobody (no live region).

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "What accessibility issues do you see here?"
- "How do you test for accessibility?"
- "How do you handle an accessible design when the deadline is tight?"

**For the deadline question** — this tests judgement, not virtue: "I would not treat it as an all-or-nothing choice. The highest-damage failures are cheap to prevent at design time: contrast, visible focus, keyboard path, real labels, and target size cost nothing extra if they are in the spec from the start. What I would triage is the expensive work — a full screen-reader audit of a complex custom component, or user testing with assistive-technology users — and I would schedule that rather than skip it, log the known gaps explicitly, and fix the criteria that block people entirely before the ones that merely inconvenience. What I would not do is ship a keyboard trap and call it a follow-up."
