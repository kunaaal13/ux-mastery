# 4.1 The Gestalt Principles

## In one line

The mind organises visual input into wholes, automatically and before conscious thought — these principles describe the rules it uses.

Gestalt psychology (Wertheimer, Köhler, Koffka, 1920s) starts from one claim: the whole is different from the sum of its parts. For interfaces, the practical translation is that **you do not control what users see element by element; you control what groups they perceive.**

---

## Law of Prägnanz (the umbrella)

The eye resolves ambiguity toward the **simplest, most stable interpretation available**. Every principle below is a specific case of this.

Design consequence: if a layout supports two readings, users take the simpler one. Your job is to make the intended reading the simplest one.

---

## 1. Proximity

**Statement:** elements close together are perceived as a group, regardless of their similarity.

The strongest of all the principles. Spacing beats colour, borders, and labels for communicating grouping.

**Application:**
- A form label must be closer to its own field than to the field above it. This single rule fixes a large share of confusing forms.
- Related actions cluster; unrelated actions get a gap.
- White space between sections should exceed white space within them. If the two are equal, the section headers are decorative and the eye reads one long list.

**Failure symptom:** users associate a helper text with the wrong field, or read two sections as one.

---

## 2. Similarity

**Statement:** elements sharing visual characteristics (colour, shape, size, orientation, texture) are perceived as related.

**Application:**
- All links look the same; all primary buttons look the same. Similarity is the mechanism behind consistency.
- Similar things that behave differently is the cardinal sin — two identical-looking chips where one filters and one deletes.
- Conversely, if two things differ in behaviour, they must differ in appearance.

**Failure symptom:** users click something expecting the behaviour of its look-alike.

---

## 3. Closure

**Statement:** the mind completes incomplete shapes, filling gaps to perceive a whole.

**Application:**
- Icons can omit strokes and still read (the classic examples are logos built from negative space).
- A card cut off at the viewport edge signals "scroll for more" precisely because the eye completes it — this is why a peeking card is a better carousel affordance than dots.
- Loading skeletons work partly through closure: the outline implies the content to come.

---

## 4. Continuity (Good Continuation)

**Statement:** the eye follows the smoothest path; elements arranged on a line or curve are seen as related.

**Application:**
- **Alignment is continuity.** A shared left edge creates an invisible line that binds a list together. Breaking alignment breaks the group.
- Horizontal scroll rows read as one set because they share a baseline.
- Connector lines in flows, steppers, and org charts exploit continuity directly.

**Failure symptom:** ragged left edges make a form feel disordered even when spacing is correct.

---

## 5. Common Region

**Statement:** elements inside a shared boundary are perceived as a group — and this **overrides proximity**.

**Application:**
- Cards. A card is common region made concrete, which is why cards are such a durable pattern: they group reliably even when spacing is tight.
- Background tints, panels, and inset containers group without borders.
- Because it overrides proximity, a card drawn around the wrong set of elements will beat correct spacing. Draw boundaries carefully.

---

## 6. Common Fate

**Statement:** elements moving in the same direction are perceived as related.

**Application:**
- Items that animate in together are read as one set.
- A row of items sliding as a unit reads as a carousel; items sliding independently read as unrelated.
- Parallax separates layers by giving them different fates — foreground and background become distinct planes.

Because motion is a pre-attentive attribute (Module 02), common fate is powerful and should be used sparingly.

---

## 7. Figure / Ground

**Statement:** the eye separates a scene into a subject (figure) and a background (ground).

**Application:**
- Modals: the scrim pushes the page to ground and the dialog to figure. A weak scrim leaves an ambiguous figure/ground relationship and the modal feels unattached.
- Elevation, shadow, and blur are figure/ground tools.
- **Ambiguous figure/ground is a bug:** if a user cannot tell whether a panel is on top of or behind the content, interaction expectations break.

---

## 8. Symmetry & Order

**Statement:** symmetrical elements are perceived as belonging together and as a coherent, stable whole.

**Application:**
- Symmetric layouts feel stable, formal, calm; asymmetric layouts feel dynamic and direct attention.
- Symmetry aids comparison: pricing tables and A/B comparisons rely on it.

---

## 9. Uniform Connectedness

**Statement:** elements connected by a visible link (a line, a shared container, a connector) are perceived as more related than elements merely near or similar. This is the **strongest** grouping cue of all.

**Application:**
- Steppers with connecting lines.
- A toolbar with grouped segmented controls sharing an outline.
- Table row striping connects a row across a wide screen.

**Grouping strength, roughly, from strongest to weakest:** uniform connectedness → common region → proximity → similarity. Knowing this ordering lets you resolve conflicts: if your card boundary and your spacing disagree, the boundary wins.

---

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "Critique this layout."
- "Why does this screen feel cluttered?"
- "What is the most important spacing rule in a form?"

**How to use Gestalt in a critique.** Narrate perception before opinion: "My eye groups these three things because they share a background, but the third belongs with the section below — so common region is fighting the actual content structure. Inside the form, the labels sit equidistant between fields, so proximity does not tell me which label belongs to which input."

That is far stronger than "it feels cluttered," and it points directly at a fix.

**Cluttered = grouping failure.** Nine times out of ten, "cluttered" means spacing is uniform (no proximity signal), alignment is ragged (no continuity), and everything shares similar visual weight (no figure/ground). Say that, and clutter becomes a solvable problem instead of a taste complaint.
