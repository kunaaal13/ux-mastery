# 7.2 States & Feedback

## In one line

A design is not finished when the happy path looks good; it is finished when every state is designed — and the states you skip are exactly where users get stuck.

## The state matrix

For any screen or component, design all of these:

| State | Question it answers | Common failure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Empty (first use)** | What is this and how do I start? | A blank area with "No data" |
| **Empty (user-cleared)** | Did I do something wrong? | Same message as first-use, which reads as a bug |
| **Empty (no results)** | Why did my search fail, what next? | "0 results" with no suggestion to relax filters |
| **Loading** | Is it working, how long? | Blocking spinner for a partial update |
| **Partial / streaming** | What is already usable? | Blocking everything until all data arrives |
| **Ideal / populated** | The normal case | The only state anyone designed |
| **Overflowing** | Long names, many items, big numbers | Broken layout, truncated identity |
| **Error (recoverable)** | What happened, how do I fix it? | "Something went wrong" |
| **Error (unrecoverable)** | What now? | A dead end with no route out |
| **Offline / degraded** | What still works? | Silent failure that looks like the empty state |
| **Success / completion** | Did it work, what next? | Nothing at all |
| **Permission denied** | Why can I not do this, who can help? | A disabled control with no explanation |

Interactive component states are a separate axis: **default, hover, focus, active/pressed, selected, disabled, loading, error, read-only**. Focus is the one most often skipped, and skipping it makes the product keyboard-unusable (Module 10).

## Empty states are the highest-leverage screen you will ignore

A first-use empty state is the only moment when you have the user's full attention and no content competing for it. A good one does three things:

1. **Explains the value** in one line ("Track every subscription in one place").
2. **Shows what filled looks like** — an illustration or sample rows beat abstract description.
3. **Gives one primary action** to get out of the empty state.

Distinguish the three empty states. "No results for 'blue jacket'" needs a different treatment (relax filters, correct spelling, browse alternatives) from "You have not added anything yet" (teach and invite).

## Feedback rules

- **Acknowledge within 100 ms.** Every tap gets an immediate visible response even if the result takes seconds (Doherty, Module 03).
- **Proportional feedback** (Shneiderman): frequent minor actions get subtle feedback; rare major actions get substantial, unmissable confirmation.
- **Feedback near the locus of attention.** A toast in the opposite corner from the click is routinely missed (change blindness, Module 02).
- **Persistent for consequential outcomes.** A toast that disappears in 4 seconds is the wrong carrier for "your payment failed."
- **Toast, inline, banner, modal** — pick by severity and required action:

| Carrier | Use for |
| --- | --- |
| Inline (at the element) | Validation, per-item results |
| Toast/snackbar | Transient success, undo affordance |
| Banner (persistent, in-page) | Ongoing conditions: degraded service, unsaved changes, trial expiring |
| Modal | Only when the user must decide before continuing |
| Full page | Unrecoverable errors, or a state that replaces the task |

Modals interrupt; use them when interruption is the point, and never for messages the user cannot act on.

## Undo over confirm

A confirmation dialog costs every user a step to protect against a rare mistake, and users learn to dismiss it reflexively — after which it protects nobody. Undo costs nothing on the happy path and actually works. Use confirmation when the action is truly irreversible, has wide blast radius, or is high-cost (deleting an account, sending to thousands of people) — and make the confirmation informative rather than generic ("Delete 1,240 records permanently" beats "Are you sure?").

## Optimistic vs. pessimistic updates

- **Optimistic** (show the result immediately, reconcile later) — right for high-success, low-cost actions: likes, reorders, marking read.
- **Pessimistic** (wait for confirmation) — right when failure matters and reversal is confusing: payments, irreversible deletes, anything with legal or financial consequence.
- Optimistic UI requires a designed **failure path**: the item reverting with an explanation, not silently.

## Applications

**Do this / not that**
- *Not that:* Deleting a row, showing a toast at the top of the page, and leaving the list unchanged until refresh.
- *Do this:* Remove the row immediately with a brief animation, show an inline "Deleted — Undo" in its place for a few seconds, and reconcile with the server in the background.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "What states would you design for this component?"
- "How do you handle errors?"
- "When would you use a modal?"

**The state matrix is a ready-made answer** and it is the fastest way to sound thorough. When asked to design anything in an interview, say: "Before I go further, let me name the states — empty first-use, empty after clearing, no results, loading, partial, populated, overflowing, error, offline, success, and permission-denied — and then I will focus on the two or three that carry the most risk here." Interviewers rarely hear the full list.

**For modals:** "Only when the user must decide before continuing, and when the decision cannot be deferred. Everything else is a banner, an inline message, or a toast. Modals block, they break keyboard and screen-reader flow if built carelessly, and they train dismissal reflexes — so each one should have to justify its existence."
