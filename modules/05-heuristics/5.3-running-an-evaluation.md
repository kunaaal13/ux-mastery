# 5.3 Running an Evaluation

## In one line

A heuristic evaluation is a structured expert inspection that finds usability problems **without users** — cheap, fast, and no substitute for testing.

## Heuristic evaluation: the method

1. **Define scope and users.** Which flows, on which platform, for which user type. An evaluation without a defined user is an opinion.
2. **Choose the heuristic set** and agree it in advance (Nielsen's 10 by default).
3. **Two passes, individually.**
   - Pass 1: free exploration to build a model of the system.
   - Pass 2: walk specific task flows, logging problems.
4. **Use 3–5 evaluators independently.** A single evaluator finds roughly a third of problems; the curve rises steeply to about five, then flattens. Independence matters — evaluators who discuss first converge and miss things.
5. **Aggregate and de-duplicate.**
6. **Rate severity** collectively.
7. **Report with fixes**, sorted by severity.

**What it is good for:** fast, cheap coverage; finding obvious violations before spending user-testing time on them; getting a baseline on a competitor.

**What it cannot do:** tell you whether users *want* the thing, uncover domain-specific misunderstandings, or predict real-world behaviour. Experts systematically over-report minor issues and under-report problems that only appear when a real user brings real goals and real data. **Run heuristic evaluation before user testing, not instead of it.**

## Severity rating

Nielsen's 0–4 scale:

| Rating | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | Not a usability problem |
| 1 | Cosmetic — fix only if time permits |
| 2 | Minor — low priority |
| 3 | Major — important to fix, high priority |
| 4 | Catastrophe — must fix before release |

Severity is a function of three factors: **frequency** (how often it occurs), **impact** (how hard it is to overcome), and **persistence** (a one-off learnable annoyance versus a repeated obstacle). A rare but unrecoverable data-loss bug outranks a constant cosmetic misalignment.

A practical prioritisation formula for reporting:

```
priority = severity × frequency-of-affected-task × (1 / cost-to-fix)
```

Do not present this as science; present it as a way to make the trade-off explicit with the PM in the room.

## Cognitive walkthrough

A complementary method focused on **learnability** for first-time users. For each step in a task, four questions:

1. Will the user try to achieve the right effect? (Do they have the right goal at this point?)
2. Will they notice the correct action is available?
3. Will they associate the correct action with the effect they want?
4. If the correct action is performed, will they see progress toward their goal?

Any "no" is a finding, and the numbered question tells you the fix type: (1) a goal/expectation problem, (2) a visibility/discoverability problem, (3) a labelling/mapping problem, (4) a feedback problem.

This maps beautifully onto Norman's gulfs — 1–3 are the Gulf of Execution, 4 is the Gulf of Evaluation. Using it live in an interview is a strong move because it is systematic and shows you can diagnose rather than merely react.

## The 20-minute app critique format

For interviews and quick reviews:

1. **Minute 0–2 — Frame.** "Who is this for, what is the primary task, and what does the business need from it?" State your assumptions out loud.
2. **Minute 2–5 — Walk the happy path** as a first-time user, narrating perception: what draws the eye, what you expect, what you actually get.
3. **Minute 5–12 — Probe the edges.** Empty state, error state, loading state, long content, offline, small screen, accessibility (keyboard, contrast, target size).
4. **Minute 12–16 — Categorise findings** by heuristic and rate severity.
5. **Minute 16–19 — Prioritise and propose.** Top three problems, a fix for each, and what you would measure.
6. **Minute 19–20 — Name what you would validate**, and what you could not assess without data or users.

Step 6 is the one candidates skip and the one interviewers remember.

## Reporting findings

A finding is not useful unless it has five parts:

| Part | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Where** | Checkout, step 2 — payment |
| **What (observed)** | Card errors appear only after submit, and the form clears |
| **Heuristic** | #9 recover from errors; #3 user control |
| **Consequence** | Users retype everything; abandonment concentrates here |
| **Severity** | 4 — catastrophic, blocks a revenue path |
| **Fix** | Validate inline on blur, preserve all entered values, keep focus on the failing field |

Sort the report by severity, never by screen order. Lead with a one-page summary of the top five problems — most stakeholders read only that page.

## Common evaluator mistakes

- **Personal preference dressed as a heuristic.** "I do not like the green" is not #4 Consistency. Say what breaks and for whom.
- **Redesigning instead of diagnosing.** Present the problem before the solution, or the discussion becomes about your taste.
- **Ignoring constraints.** Recommending a full IA overhaul for a team with two weeks produces nothing. Include a cheap fix alongside the right fix.
- **Only reviewing the happy path.** Most severe problems live in error, empty, offline, and long-content states.
- **No severity ratings.** A flat list of 40 problems is unusable and gets ignored.

## Interview angle

**Common questions**
- "Critique this app." (Whole round at many companies.)
- "How would you evaluate a design without any users?"
- "How do you prioritise usability issues?"

**For "evaluate without users"** — name the method properly: "A heuristic evaluation with 3–5 independent evaluators against an agreed set, plus a cognitive walkthrough for the first-run experience, plus analytics and support tickets as proxy evidence. Then I would be explicit about the limits: expert inspection over-reports cosmetic issues and cannot tell me whether the concept is wanted at all, so I would frame the output as hypotheses ranked by severity, not as facts."

**For prioritisation** — severity (frequency × impact × persistence), weighted by how central the affected task is, then traded against fix cost with engineering in the room. Say explicitly that data loss and revenue-blocking failures outrank everything cosmetic regardless of count.
